## 1. Emotional display rules and emotional labor (Hochschild, Grandey); surface vs. deep acting and their costs

### Takeaway
The meta-analytic evidence is consistent: **surface acting** (faking or suppressing what you show while the feeling stays the same) goes with impaired well-being, including burnout-type strain. **Deep acting** (changing the feeling itself, for example by reappraisal) is only weakly linked to strain and is positively linked to how well people perform emotionally. If a coach teaches people to "put on a face", it is teaching the costly strategy.

### Cited Findings
- Hülsheger & Schewe (2011), *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* 16(3), 361–389: a meta-analysis of 494 correlations from 95 independent studies covering three decades of emotional-labor research. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21728441/); [Maastricht repository PDF](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/ws/files/74901261/Hulsheger_2011_On_the_costs_and_benefits_of_emotional.pdf)
- Emotion-rule dissonance and surface acting showed "substantial relationships" with indicators of impaired well-being and with job attitudes. Deep acting showed weak relationships with impaired well-being and job attitudes, but positive relationships with emotional performance and customer outcomes. [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21728441/)
- Surface acting partly mediated the link between emotional-labor demands and outcomes, which the authors read as support for a mediated process model. [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21728441/)
- Huppertz, Hülsheger and colleagues (2020) summarize the field this way: surface acting "has repeatedly been found to harm employee well-being", while findings for deep acting are "weak or inconsistent". In their data, surface acting had significant indirect links to **emotional exhaustion** through two paths, **psychological effort** and **reduced felt authenticity**. [Maastricht University record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/why-do-emotional-labor-strategies-differentially-predict-exhausti/)
- A secondary summary says deep acting may also be positively linked to some psychosomatic stress and exhaustion symptoms, so it is not cost-free. Snippet only. [Aston repository record](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/document/72591)
- Related cost found in a non-service setting: the strain of keeping cameras on in video meetings was attributed to "self-presentation costs" and was larger for women and newer employees (details in section 6). [University of Georgia news](https://news.uga.edu/cameras-not-meetings-cause-zoom-fatigue/)
- Related cost of hiding traits: in a UK study, exhaustion was a common result of workplace masking among autistic, other neurodivergent and neurotypical adults (details in section 5). [PMC10482295](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10482295/)

### Inferences
- If Steady nudges users to suppress or hide what they show ("don't look frustrated"), it may lower short-term visible risk while increasing exhaustion and inauthenticity. That is exactly the surface-acting profile. Coaching aimed at regulating the feeling (reappraisal, preparing before a meeting, a pause before replying), or at expressing the feeling in a way that fits the setting, matches the less costly deep-acting profile better.
- The deep-acting benefits (emotional performance, customer outcomes) were measured mostly in service and customer-facing work. Carrying them over to peer meetings in knowledge work is reasonable but has not been shown directly in the material I found.
- The felt-authenticity mechanism matters for product design. Feedback that makes users feel they are performing a fake self may cause harm through that same path.

### Gaps
- I retrieved no source for **Hochschild (1983), *The Managed Heart*** (the origin of "emotional labor" and of "surface" vs. "deep" acting) or for **Grandey (2000)**, *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* (which places emotional labor inside Gross's emotion-regulation model). Both are foundational, and the report writer should cite them from the primary works.
- I did not get exhaustion-specific effect sizes from Hülsheger & Schewe (2011). The full text is at doi:10.1037/a0022876.
- I found no emotional-labor studies aimed specifically at office or knowledge workers dealing with peers and managers, as opposed to customers.

## 2. How anger, frustration, crying, anxiety, defensiveness and contempt affect perceived competence, warmth, leadership potential, status and ratings (including gender, race, age, accent)

### Takeaway
**Tiedens (2001)** found that anger, compared with sadness, earned more status through higher perceived competence. **Brescoll & Uhlmann (2008)** found that this benefit went to men and not to women. The later record is **mixed and partly contradictory**. A 2024 replication found anger lowered status for everyone. A 2021 study found angry women were rewarded. The experimental evidence for a race-by-gender penalty, with Black women's anger read as a personal trait (Motro et al., 2022), is fairly strong. Crying lowers perceived competence, and men pay a larger penalty for it. Penalties from accent come mainly with heavy accents and have replicated inconsistently. I found little rigorous work on anxiety, defensiveness or contempt in workplace evaluations specifically.

### Cited Findings
**Anger and status (Tiedens and follow-ups)**
- Tiedens (2001), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 80(1), 86–94, four studies: expressing anger rather than sadness led observers to give the expresser more status. Perceived competence mediated the effect. A job applicant who expressed anger was rated more competent, given more status and recommended for higher pay. [MIT-hosted PDF](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Affect/Tiedens.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Anger-and-advancement-versus-sadness-and-the-effect-Tiedens/bb6e3394cfd27199c01e45df0b4edd459fe651b7)
- Even in this line of work, angry expressers were rated as colder, less friendly and less likable. Anger buys perceived competence and status at a cost in warmth (per the 2024 Frontiers paper's literature summary, snippet only). [Frontiers in Social Psychology 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1337715/full)
- A 2024 *Frontiers in Social Psychology* paper copied the Tiedens (2001) and Brescoll & Uhlmann (2008) paradigms more exactly. It found angry workers were penalized and given **less** status than sad or emotionless workers, and argued that anger is not instrumental for gaining status at work. Snippet only; the full text could not be fetched. [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1337715/full)
- **Status: contested.** The search turned up no large, preregistered direct replication of either Tiedens (2001) or Brescoll & Uhlmann (2008). [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1337715/full); [SAGE record, Brescoll & Uhlmann](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02079.x)

**Gender**
- Brescoll & Uhlmann (2008), *Psychological Science*: men who expressed anger at work were given more status than men who expressed sadness. Both male and female evaluators gave **angry female professionals less status than angry male professionals**. When the woman's anger was given an **external reason**, the gender bias disappeared. A footnote reports the result replicated when participants read transcripts instead of watching videos. [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02079.x); [HKS-hosted PDF](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/wappp_files/pdfs/brescoll_emotion_workpalce.pdf)
- Contradicting result: McCormick-Huhn & Shields (2021), *Frontiers in Psychology*, "Favorable Evaluations of Black and White Women's Workplace Anger During the Era of #MeToo", found angry women given **more** status than angry men. [PMC7947812](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7947812/)
- Another *Frontiers in Psychology* paper, titled "Workplace Anger Costs Women Irrespective of Race", was described as a conceptual replication of Brescoll & Uhlmann extended to Black women. I verified the title and framing only. [PMC7677348](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7677348/)

**Race and gender together**
- Motro, Evans, Ellis & Benson (2022), *Journal of Applied Psychology* 107(1), 142–152, doi:10.1037/apl0000884, two studies, total n = 555. Observers were more likely to attribute anger to internal traits when the angry person was a **Black woman**. This led to **lower performance evaluations and lower ratings of leadership capability**. Study 2 replicated the result and supported stereotype activation as the mechanism. [Library record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_2508570406); [Ethical Psychology summary](https://www.ethicalpsychology.com/2022/04/race-and-reactions-to-womens.html)
- There was no difference between Black and white men, and observers of all races and genders showed the effect to a similar degree. The design used actors on video receiving negative feedback, so it is an experiment rather than a field study. [UBC summary](https://beyond.ubc.ca/how-racism-holds-black-women-back-from-leadership-positions/)
- Livingston, Rosette & Washington (2012), *Psychological Science*, which looked at agentic dominance rather than anger: white women and Black men in leader roles were given less status when they acted dominant rather than communal, but Black women and white men were not. The authors stressed that this does **not** mean Black women escape workplace prejudice, and suggested they may face a larger penalty for mistakes. [HKS](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/can-agentic-black-woman-get-ahead-impact-race-and-interpersonal-dominance-perceptions); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-do-people-perceive-dominant-behaviour-black-female-leaders); [Kellogg Insight](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/do_black_women_have_more_or_less_freedom_as_leaders)
- Rosette et al. (2016), *Leadership Quarterly*: whether a woman leader is penalized depends on which kind of agency is involved. They separate a competence-based "agentic deficiency" from a dominance-based "agentic penalty", and race changes which one applies. [HKS](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/race-matters-women-leaders-intersectional-effects-agentic-deficiencies-and-penalties)

**Crying and sadness**
- Motro & Ellis (2017), *Journal of Applied Psychology*, "Boys, Don't Cry: Gender and Reactions to Negative Performance Feedback", 169 participants watching videos of employees getting negative reviews. **Men who cried** were seen as significantly atypical and rated lower on competence and fitness for leadership. Women who cried showed no significant penalty on these measures. [Eller College news](https://executive.eller.arizona.edu/news/2017/04/boys-don%E2%80%99t-cry); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/men-study-suggests-its-really-bad-idea-cry-front-your-colleagues)
- In a study of tearful faces, seeing tears raised perceived sadness, which partly explained **lower perceived competence** of the person crying. [PMC5363367](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5363367)
- Framing helps: when an upset person described themselves as "passionate" rather than "emotional", they were seen as more competent (HBS-linked research summarized in Working Knowledge). [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/the-right-way-to-cry-in-front-of-your-boss)

**Theory of how observers read emotions**
- Van Kleef and colleagues' EASI (Emotions as Social Information) framework treats emotional expressions as social information that shapes how observers respond at work. It is the main theory for why the same display can help in one setting and hurt in another. [Van Kleef, Homan & Cheshin 2012, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/2041386612454911); [Annual Review, "The Social Effects of Emotions"](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-020821-010855)

**Accent**
- Lev-Ari & Keysar (2010), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*: trivia statements spoken with a **heavy** foreign accent were rated less truthful. Statements with a **mild** accent were rated as truthful as native speech. The authors blame processing difficulty that listeners misattribute to the content, not prejudice; participants were told speakers were only reading a native speaker's statements. [Max Planck PDF](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_1837436_6/component/file_1837698/LevAriKeysar.pdf); [UChicago News](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/foreign-accents-make-speakers-seem-less-truthful-listeners-research-shows)
- **Replication is mixed.** Souza & Markman did not replicate the effect and neither did a second experiment by Stocker; Boduch-Grabka & Lev-Ari did reproduce it. The applications to job seekers and call centers were the authors' extrapolation, not tested in the study. [PMC10885845 (illusory-truth study and literature review)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10885845/)

### Inferences
- **Treat anger as risky for everyone, and more so for some groups.** The newest exact replication (2024) found anger cost status overall, and the trade of warmth for competence shows up even in Tiedens' original data. Steady should not teach that anger "works". It also should not tell women or Black women to suppress more than other people. Doing that would build the double standard into the product.
- **Attribution is a practical lever.** In Brescoll & Uhlmann (2008), an external reason for the anger removed the gender penalty. In Motro et al. (2022), the penalty ran through internal (trait) attributions. Coaching that helps users name the situation behind a feeling ("I'm frustrated because the deadline moved twice") rather than letting it read as temperament fits the mechanism found in both studies. The same goes for the "passion" framing for tears.
- **Crying penalties run against gender stereotypes.** Men are penalized more in the one controlled study I found. Advice about crying should not be split by gender in ways that reinforce stereotypes.
- **Accent features are risky.** Any feature that scores "clarity" or fluency could encode accent bias. The evidence that accent lowers credibility is weak and mixed for mild accents, so there is little basis for pushing users toward accent change.

### Gaps
- **Anxiety, defensiveness and contempt.** I retrieved no workplace evaluation studies on visible nervousness or anxiety, defensive responses to feedback, or contempt signals such as eye-rolls and sarcasm. These need targeted searches; the interview-anxiety literature is one lead.
- **Age.** I found no sources on how age changes judgments of emotional displays at work, for example older workers seen as "rigid" or younger workers as "emotional".
- **Field evidence.** Almost every study here is a short lab or online experiment using actors, videos or vignettes. I found no field studies linking real displays to real performance ratings or promotions.
- **Effect sizes.** None were verified, because no full texts could be fetched.
- I did not find a Van Kleef meta-analysis specifically on anger and status, and the search could not confirm that one exists.
- Unverified leads for the report writer: Salerno & Peter-Hagene (2015) "One angry woman" (jury deliberation); Geddes & Callister (2007) "dual threshold model" of workplace anger; Glomb & Hulin (1997) on angry supervisors. I recall these from background knowledge only and did not verify them in this session.

## 3. Behaviors rated positively (composure, executive presence, listening, warmth plus competence, constructive disagreement, EI) and the strength of evidence for EI predicting performance

### Takeaway
Emotional intelligence (EI) correlates moderately with job performance (corrected ρ ≈ .24–.30). For self-report "mixed" EI, most of that link disappears once personality, cognitive ability and self-efficacy are controlled. Ability EI predicts task performance mainly in jobs high in emotional labor. "Executive presence" is mostly a practitioner construct: survey-based, with gravitas at its core, and people of color report being held to a stricter version of it. I found little peer-reviewed workplace evidence in this session on composure, active listening or constructive disagreement specifically.

### Cited Findings
**Emotional intelligence and performance**
- O'Boyle et al. (2011), *Journal of Organizational Behavior* meta-analysis: the three EI "streams" (ability-based, self-report, mixed) had corrected correlations of **.24 to .30** with job performance. Streams 2 and 3 (self-report and mixed) showed the most incremental validity beyond cognitive ability and the Five Factor Model. [CiNii record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1363388844153142912?lang=en)
- According to a secondary account, the ability-EI correlation with task performance was about .21 but fell to about .07 once other individual differences were controlled. [CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/216952545.pdf)
- Joseph & Newman (2010), per secondary accounts: ability EI had a meta-analytic correlation of about .16 with performance. Ability EI **did not** predict task performance once personality and cognitive ability were controlled, and it predicted performance only in jobs requiring high emotional labor. [CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/216952545.pdf); [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10561035)
- Joseph, Jin, Newman & O'Boyle (2015), *Journal of Applied Psychology*: mixed (self-report) EI correlated **ρ = .29** with supervisor-rated performance, but the relationship became **nil (β = −.02)** after controlling for ability EI, self-efficacy, the Big Five and related covariates. [University of Illinois Experts](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/why-does-self-reported-emotional-intelligence-predict-job-perform/); [UCF STARS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/facultybib2010/6608)

**Executive presence (practitioner research)**
- In the Center for Talent Innovation's 2012 research (now Coqual), 67% of 268 senior executives named **gravitas** as the core of executive presence. 28% named communication and 5% named appearance. [Coqual key findings PDF](https://coqual.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/26_executivepresence_keyfindings-1.pdf)
- Secondary summaries say the CTI survey reached about 4,000 college-educated professionals. Women and people of color reported contradictory, hard-to-act-on feedback about their "presence". People of color felt held to a stricter standard than white colleagues, about **56% vs. 31%**. Not verified against the primary report. [Coqual key findings PDF](https://coqual.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/26_executivepresence_keyfindings-1.pdf); [Tandem Coach critique](https://tandemcoach.co/sylvia-hewlett-executive-presence/)
- Hewlett published *Executive Presence 2.0: Leadership in an Age of Inclusion* in 2024, updating the framework. [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/123279497-executive-presence-2-0)
- An autistic leader's commentary describes executive presence as "the interface no one designed". This is opinion, not research, but it captures the neurodivergent critique. [The Autistic Leader (Substack)](https://theautisticleader.substack.com/p/writing-interface-invisibility)

**Calm vs. excited positivity**
- What counts as positive affect for leaders and job applicants varies by culture. US and European-American observers favor excited, high-arousal positivity, and East Asian observers favor calm (see section 7). [Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2016/02/smiles-jeanne-tsai-021516)

### Inferences
- The EI evidence does not support marketing claims that "EI predicts success" as a skill apart from personality and ability. A more defensible product claim is narrower: specific regulation and communication behaviors reduce specific costs, such as incivility (section 4) or anger read as a trait (section 2).
- "Executive presence" is a vague, subjective standard. Coqual's own data show it is applied more strictly to people of color. Steady should avoid a "presence score" and favor concrete, describable behaviors the user chooses, such as stating the reason behind a disagreement or summarizing what someone else said before replying.
- Ability EI matters more in jobs heavy on emotional labor. That supports targeting regulation help at moments of high emotional demand, such as conflict or receiving feedback, rather than general "EI training".

### Gaps
- I retrieved no peer-reviewed workplace sources on **composure under pressure**, **active listening** (for example Itzchakov and Kluger's listening research), **constructive disagreement or voice framing**, or **warmth plus competence** (Fiske's Stereotype Content Model; Cuddy, Kohut & Neffinger's "Connect, then lead"). These are well-known literatures, but they are not sourced here.
- I did not retrieve the full O'Boyle (2011) or Joseph & Newman (2010) texts, so the reported figures come from secondary sources.
- I did not search for Mayer–Salovey–Caruso EI test validity debates or for newer (2020–2026) EI meta-analyses.

## 4. Workplace incivility and its costs (Porath and Pearson), psychological safety (Edmondson), single outbursts, and recovery or apology

### Takeaway
Incivility is common (98% report having experienced it) and costly. People who are targets cut their effort, lose time worrying and sometimes quit. Psychological safety predicts team learning and is linked to team effectiveness. I found **no** retrievable research on how one outburst affects reputation, or on how an apology repairs it. That is a major gap for Steady.

### Cited Findings
- Porath & Pearson, poll of about **800 managers and employees across 17 industries** (*HBR*, "The Price of Incivility", 2013): among people treated rudely, **48% deliberately cut their work effort** and **80% lost work time worrying** about the incident. [McKinsey Quarterly, "The hidden toll of workplace incivility" (2016)](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organization/our-insights/the-hidden-toll-of-workplace-incivility); [Georgetown McDonough](https://msb.georgetown.edu/news-story/civil-actions/)
- NPR's account of the same research: 66% said their performance declined, 80% lost time worrying, and **12% left their jobs**. Figures differ between write-ups, so check the original HBR article before quoting. [NPR transcript](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/686338396)
- Porath's 2016 survey: **98%** of workers had experienced incivility at work and **62%** experienced it at least monthly. This is a different survey from the 800-person poll and should not be merged with it. [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/from-functioning-to-flourishing/201707/why-are-our-workplaces-getting-ruder); [McKinsey PDF](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/McKinsey/Business%20Functions/Organization/Our%20Insights/The%20hidden%20toll%20of%20workplace%20incivility/The-hidden-toll-of-workplace-incivility-Dec-2016.pdf)
- Edmondson (1999), *Administrative Science Quarterly*: team psychological safety predicted team learning behavior, which in turn predicted performance. Secondary sources only. [Psych Safety](https://psychsafety.com/googles-project-aristotle/); [Yu-kai Chou summary](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-analysis/psychological-safety-edmondson-team-learning/)
- Google's Project Aristotle (around 2012–2015, 180+ teams) reportedly found psychological safety was the strongest predictor of team effectiveness and borrowed Edmondson's term. Sources give the publication date as either 2015 or 2016. **Secondary and blog sources only**; I did not reach Google's re:Work page. [Psych Safety](https://psychsafety.com/googles-project-aristotle/); [Improv.eu](https://improv.eu/blog/project-aristotle-psychological-safety)

### Inferences
- Incivility is the best-measured cost in this whole area, and the costs fall on **other people**: colleagues' effort, time and retention. For Steady, this supports prioritizing behaviors that reliably hurt others no matter who does them, such as rudeness, dismissiveness, belittling and contempt. That is a safer basis than appearance norms like how expressive your face is.
- A team's psychological safety depends partly on how its members, and especially leaders, react to mistakes and dissent. A user who is working on reactivity improves the team climate, not only their own reputation.

### Gaps
- **Single outburst and reputation.** I found no empirical source on how one emotional outburst changes a person's reputation or ratings over time, or on asymmetry effects such as negativity bias in impressions.
- **Recovery and apology.** I found no source on apology or repair after an emotional display at work. Unverified leads: Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks (2004) on apology vs. denial in trust repair (competence vs. integrity violations); Geddes & Stickney (2011) on how others' responses after workplace anger affect outcomes. Background knowledge only, not checked.
- **Witnesses.** Data on how incivility affects bystanders or witnesses was not retrieved.
- The original HBR article (Porath & Pearson 2013) and Edmondson (1999) were not reached directly.

## 5. Neurodiversity and disability: misjudgment of autistic, ADHD, anxious or traumatized employees; costs of masking; accommodation (ADA)

### Takeaway
Within seconds, observers form less favorable impressions of autistic adults (less likable, more awkward), yet rate them the same on trustworthiness and intelligence. The bias comes from how people present, not from what they say: it disappears with transcripts and shrinks with disclosure and autism knowledge. Masking to avoid these judgments goes with exhaustion across groups and with suicidality among autistic people. Under the ADA, accommodations such as advance agendas are available, but conduct and performance standards generally still apply.

### Cited Findings
- Sasson et al. (2017), *Scientific Reports*, doi:10.1038/srep40700: from brief "thin slice" videos, observers rated autistic adults the same as non-autistic adults on **trustworthiness and intelligence**, but less favorably on **likeability and awkwardness**, and were **less willing to interact** with them. The biases **disappeared when impressions were based on transcripts** (content without audio-visual cues). [Nature / Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep40700); [PMC5286449](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5286449)
- Sasson & Morrison, *Autism* (SAGE; online 2017): first impressions of intellectually able autistic adults improved when the diagnosis was disclosed and when peers knew more about autism. [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362361317729526)
- UK workplace masking study (285 autistic, 88 non-autistic neurodivergent, 99 neurotypical adults): **exhaustion** was a common result of workplace masking in all groups. Masking leading to **suicidal ideation** appeared more specific to autistic participants. Participants saw masking as protection against negative social and employment outcomes. [PMC10482295](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10482295/)
- 2026 systematic review (2015–2025 literature) of autistic women: masking was consistently associated with anxiety and depression and was an **independent predictor of suicidality**. Most of the underlying evidence is correlational. [Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-026-07557-0)
- The Job Accommodation Network (JAN) offers free, confidential guidance on workplace accommodations and the ADA. Its autism guidance includes giving advance notice of meeting topics. [JAN](https://askjan.org:443/); [JAN sitemap](https://askjan.org/sitemap.cfm)
- SHRM recommends sending meeting agendas and setting expectations ahead of time for neurodivergent employees, including in remote work. [SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/employee-relations/neurodiverse-employees-may-need-accommodations-remote-work)
- Employers must provide reasonable accommodation for qualifying disabilities, which can include autism. [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/reasonable-accommodations-employees-autism-practical-guide?amp)
- A JAN-linked source says reasonable accommodation does not require lowering performance standards or removing essential job functions. Snippet; exact source page not confirmed. [University of Utah accommodations PDF](https://unwp.utah.edu/_resources/documents/accommodations-compliance.pdf)
- An ADHD diagnosis that limits essential job functions can support an ADA accommodation request. [CHADD Attention article (2008, older)](https://www.chadd.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/ATTN_08_13_WorkplaceAccomodations.pdf)
- The Employer Assistance and Resource Network (EARN) publishes neurodiversity accommodation guidance covering workspace, schedules and other changes. [AskEARN](https://askearn.org/page/neurodiversity-accommodations)

### Inferences
- **Do not score atypical presentation as a deficit.** Flat affect, unusual eye contact, prosody and similar cues are exactly the channels that produce the biased thin-slice judgments, and the content is judged fine. A coach that flags these as "bad" would push autistic users toward masking, which is linked to exhaustion and suicidality risk. That is a safety issue, not only a fairness one.
- A safer design: let users turn off whole signal channels (face, gaze, prosody) and choose their own goals. Treat any cue as "how this might be read by some people", never as "wrong". Point toward environmental fixes such as agendas, written follow-ups and camera-optional norms that JAN, SHRM and EARN already recognize as accommodations.
- The transcript result suggests a constructive option. Helping users make their **content** clearer, for example structuring points or putting reasoning in writing, may close perception gaps without the user changing their nonverbal self.
- Steady itself might work as, or support, an accommodation. Any product claims or employer-facing features that touch disability need legal review.

### Gaps
- I retrieved no studies on how **ADHD** behaviors (interrupting, tangents, emotional intensity or "rejection sensitivity"), **anxiety disorders** or **trauma/PTSD** responses are judged in workplace evaluations.
- I did not reach EEOC primary guidance. From background knowledge, not verified here: EEOC guidance on the ADA and conduct standards allows employers to hold employees with disabilities to job-related conduct standards consistent with business necessity, and accommodation generally looks forward (it need not excuse past misconduct). The writer should verify this at eeoc.gov.

## 6. Video meetings: camera-on behavior, eye contact, facial expressiveness, background, self-presentation, and remote performance evaluation bias

### Takeaway
Keeping the camera on causes measurable fatigue, which reduces voice and engagement, and the effect is stronger for women and newer employees. Backgrounds shape first impressions of trustworthiness and competence. There is evidence of bias against remote or hybrid workers in promotions, driven by perceived commitment rather than measured performance. I found no peer-reviewed sources on eye contact or gaze in video calls.

### Cited Findings
- Shockley, Gabriel, Robertson & Rosen (2021), *Journal of Applied Psychology*: a 4-week within-person field experiment that manipulated camera use (103 employees, 1,408 daily observations, mostly at one health-care services firm). **Cameras on → more fatigue → less engagement and less voice in meetings.** Camera use had no direct effect on engagement or voice; the effect ran through fatigue. [University of Arizona Experts](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-fatiguing-effects-of-camera-use-in-virtual-meetings-a-within-/); [UGA News](https://news.uga.edu/cameras-not-meetings-cause-zoom-fatigue/)
- The effects were **stronger for women and for newer employees**, which the authors attribute to extra self-presentation pressure (for women, pressure to be "effortlessly perfect" and a higher chance of childcare interruptions; for newer employees, feeling they must be on camera to show productivity). The authors recommend camera **autonomy** without assumptions about productivity. Single-firm sample. [Eller College news](https://eller.arizona.edu/news/2021/08/cameras-or-research-suggests-cameras-during-virtual-meetings-lowers-fatigue); [SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/study-camera-use-virtual-meetings-leads-to-fatigue-disengagement)
- Cook, Thompson & Ross (2023), *PLOS ONE*, "Virtual first impressions: Zoom backgrounds affect judgements of trust and competence", about 167 participants aged 19–68: **plant and bookshelf** backgrounds were rated highest on both trustworthiness and competence, and **home-interior and novelty** backgrounds lowest. The person's **gender and facial expression** also affected ratings. [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0291444); [PMC10529556](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10529556/); [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2023-09-background.html)
- Remote work and promotion (Stanford/Bloom travel-agency experiment, 2015): remote workers were less likely to be promoted despite being about 13% more productive. Some sources say 15%, so the figure varies by source (older finding). [Inc.](https://www.inc.com/jeff-haden/research-shows-youre-less-likely-to-promote-remote-workers.html); [UC Davis GSM](https://gsm.ucdavis.edu/news/what-proximity-bias)
- A peer-reviewed experiment on statistical discrimination, as reported in trade press: with **no performance information**, hybrid workers had a **7.7 percentage-point lower chance of promotion** and **7.1 points lower chance of a raise** than office-based workers. Fully remote workers rated as equal performers were still seen as **less committed**, and once perceived commitment was controlled, the penalty for high-performing remote workers disappeared. Fathers and childless men working remotely were penalized on both performance and commitment. I could not identify the primary paper from the snippets. [HR Grapevine (2025)](https://www.hrgrapevine.com/us/content/article/2025-07-17-remote-workers-face-career-deevelopment-pay-penalties)
- Elsbach's "passive face time" research: simply being seen at work, regardless of output, can shape evaluations, and managers usually don't realize they're doing it. The 2010 study used 60 corporate office workers and vignettes; detailed results were not in the snippets. [UC Davis GSM](https://gsm.ucdavis.edu/news/what-proximity-bias); [UC Davis blog](https://www.ucdavis.edu/blog/curiosity/does-proximity-bias-affect-you-work)
- Physical cues such as eye contact and body language are harder to read on screen (news commentary, not research). [Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/business-and-innovation/all-news/article-760724)

### Inferences
- If Steady encourages "camera on, look engaged, stay expressive", it adds to the self-presentation load that drives fatigue and lower voice, and that load is heaviest for women and newcomers. The coach should treat camera-off as legitimate and not count it against the user.
- The remote-evaluation evidence shows that *visibility* and *perceived commitment*, not displayed emotion, drive part of the remote penalty. Help with making contributions visible in content (summaries, follow-ups) may matter more than optimizing on-camera affect.
- Facial expression affected trust and competence ratings in the background study. So video judgments do respond to expressiveness, but the evidence comes from still images and first impressions, not ongoing working relationships.

### Gaps
- **Eye contact and gaze on video.** I found no peer-reviewed studies on how looking at the camera versus the screen, or software gaze correction, affects impressions in workplace video calls.
- **Facial expressiveness over time on video.** I found no studies beyond first-impression ratings of still images.
- I found no studies on whether camera-off is penalized in actual performance ratings, as opposed to fatigue outcomes.
- The primary citation for the 7.7%/7.1% statistical-discrimination study could not be confirmed.

## 7. Cultural variation in what is seen as professional (directness, expressiveness, eye contact) across US and international workplaces

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence is Tsai's work on "ideal affect". US and European-American culture values excited, high-arousal positivity, which shows in leaders' smiles and in hiring preferences. East Asian contexts value calm. Claims about eye contact and directness across cultures are widely repeated, but in this search they came mainly from practitioner sources.

### Cited Findings
- Tsai et al. (2016), *Emotion*, "Leaders' Smiles Reflect Cultural Differences in Ideal Affect": American leaders showed more **excited** smiles than Chinese and Taiwanese leaders, whether or not they had won and regardless of rank. Across nations, the more a culture valued high-arousal positive states, the more excited smiles its leaders showed, and the reverse held for calm smiles. A nation's ideal affect predicted leaders' smiles better than its wealth or typical emotional experience. [PMC4760873](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4760873); [Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2016/02/smiles-jeanne-tsai-021516); [Stanford lab PDF](https://culture-emotion-lab.stanford.edu/sites/culture_emotion_lab/files/media/file/tsai_et_al_leaders_smiles_2016.pdf)
- Follow-up work from Tsai's lab on workplace judgments: culture and ideal affect shaped how extraverted and agreeable participants found an **excited job applicant**, which raised their likelihood of hiring that person. European Americans trusted excited partners more, while Koreans trusted calm partners more. Snippet-level only. [Jeanne Tsai, Stanford profile](https://profiles.stanford.edu/jeanne-tsai); [Psi Chi interview](https://www.psichi.org/page/242EyeWinter19Tsai); [Tsai lab manuscript on ideal affect and leader choice](https://culture-emotion-lab.stanford.edu/sites/culture_emotion_lab/files/media/file/idealaffectleaderchoicefinalaccepted103125.pdf)
- Practitioner sources, **not peer-reviewed**: direct, sustained eye contact signals confidence and sincerity in many Western business settings, but prolonged direct eye contact can seem aggressive in parts of East Asia, where averted gaze can signal respect and be misread as disinterest. [Success Across Cultures](https://successacrosscultures.com/2024/08/04/eye-contact-culture-a-guide-to-understanding-non-verbal-communication-part-ii/); [Talaera](https://www.talaera.com/speaking/nonverbal-communication-in-different-cultures/)
- Practitioner and secondary sources: collectivist cultures often favor holding back emotion to protect group harmony, while individualist cultures treat expressiveness as authenticity. Low-quality sourcing. [ResearchGate preprint, "Cultural Differences in Nonverbal Communication"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391050068_Cultural_Differences_in_Nonverbal_Communication)

### Inferences
- The "enthusiastic, high-energy, sustained eye contact" ideal of US professionalism is culturally specific, not universal. A coach trained on US norms could mark calm, restrained or gaze-averting users (often East Asian or other non-US-socialized employees) as disengaged. Any expressiveness or eye-contact signal should be optional and labeled as one norm among several.
- In multinational teams the "right" display depends on the audience. Framing feedback as "how this may land with *this* audience" is more accurate than "good vs. bad".

### Gaps
- I retrieved no peer-reviewed sources on cultural variation in **directness or disagreement norms** at work (for example Erin Meyer's *The Culture Map*, which is practitioner work; Hall's high- vs. low-context framework; GLOBE leadership studies).
- I found no peer-reviewed cross-cultural studies of **eye contact in workplace evaluations** specifically.
- I found nothing on how immigrant or international employees in **US** workplaces are evaluated for emotional display beyond the accent research in section 2.
