## 1. FACS, action units for each emotion, and how AUs map to MediaPipe/ARKit blendshapes

### Takeaway
FACS describes anatomically based facial muscle movements (action units, AUs). The "prototype" AU combinations people link to anger, disgust and other emotions are hypotheses from basic emotion theory, not validated detectors. MediaPipe's 52 blendshapes only "loosely correspond" to FACS AUs. The first published MediaPipe-to-AU mapping (2026) is an expert-consensus mapping, not a measured correlation, and some AUs (for example AU23 lip tightener) have no clean blendshape equivalent. "Frustration" has no established FACS prototype at all.

### Cited Findings
- **MediaPipe output:** The Face Landmarker blendshape model outputs 52 blendshape scores, "coefficients representing facial different expressions". Blendshape output is off by default and is turned on with `output_face_blendshapes`. The sample output includes `browDownLeft`, `browDownRight`, `browInnerUp` and `browOuterUpLeft` — [Google MediaPipe Face Landmarker guide](https://ai.google.dev/mediapipe/solutions/vision/face_landmarker); [Web guide](https://developers.google.com/edge/mediapipe/solutions/vision/face_landmarker/web_js) (snippet-verified).
- **Left/right convention:** `*Left` and `*Right` blendshapes refer to the subject's own face, not the viewer's, and a horizontally mirrored input image inverts them. This matters for selfie-camera previews, which are usually mirrored — [MediaPipe Face Landmarker docs via search summary](https://developers.google.com/edge/mediapipe/solutions/vision/face_landmarker) (snippet-verified).
- **Relationship to FACS:** A Hugging Face port of the MediaPipe blendshape model (py-feat) says the 52 blendshapes are "similar to ARKit Face Blendshapes and loosely correspond to Facial Action Unit Coding System" — [py-feat/mp_blendshapes](https://huggingface.co/py-feat/mp_blendshapes) (snippet-verified).
- **First standardized MediaPipe-to-AU mapping (2026):** Turrisi et al., "Blendshape features meet action units: a clinical mapping for enhancing facial expression analysis", *Computers in Human Behavior Reports* (2026, DOI 10.1016/j.chbr.2026.101125). It describes itself as "one of the first publicly documented efforts toward a standardized mapping between MediaPipe's 52 blendshape coefficients and AUs." Ten licensed clinical psychologists/psychotherapists built it through independent annotation and consensus, and 98% of mappings had majority (6/10) expert support. The snippets reported no statistical correlation with FACS-coded ground truth — [pre-proof PDF, IRIS CNR](https://iris.cnr.it/retrieve/b57d9fa2-3eb6-403b-8190-dbe4bbda0a16/Blendshape%20features%20meet%20action%20units%20pre-proof.pdf); [record](https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/583461) (snippet-verified).
- **Other 2026 work:** A 2026 *Sensors* paper (26(3):1060) builds an on-device emotion pipeline using "FACS-guided prototypes" on MediaPipe-style blendshape vectors and says the coefficients "can be mapped to FACS action units" — [MDPI Sensors](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/26/3/1060) (snippet-verified).
- **Blendshape spaces lose AU information:** A CVPR 2026 workshop paper (BlendME) used Meta Quest Pro blendshapes, not MediaPipe. Translating expression parameters into that blendshape space kept only about 27% of CASME II's annotated AUs, which points to substantial information loss when projecting into a blendshape space — [BlendME CVPRW 2026 supplemental](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2026W/SVC/supplemental/Sribabu_BlendME_A_VR_CVPRW_2026_supplemental.pdf) (snippet-verified; different blendshape set from MediaPipe).
- **Community ARKit-to-FACS sheet:** On an Apple Developer Forums thread asking for an ARKit-to-FACS mapping, a user links an "ARKit to FACS translation sheet" by Melinda Ozel. Apple's own ARKit documentation describes the coefficients but gives no official FACS correspondences in the snippets returned — [Apple Developer Forums thread 703686](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/703686); [ARKit to FACS cheat sheet](https://melindaozel.com/sd/arkit-to-facs-cheat-sheet/); [Apple ARFaceAnchor.blendShapes](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/arkit/arfaceanchor/blendshapes.md) (contents of the cheat sheet not retrieved).
- **Contempt is asymmetric:** The Paul Ekman Group says contempt is "the only unilateral" (asymmetrical) expression and can appear with or without a hint of a smile. It defines contempt as feeling superior to someone, usually on moral grounds — [Paul Ekman Group: What is contempt](https://paulekman.com/universal-emotions/what-is-contempt) (snippet-verified; the snippet did not name AU14 specifically).
- **Status of the AU prototypes:** Barrett et al. (2019) treat the anger "scowl" and the other prototype configurations as hypotheses and test them for reliability, specificity, generalizability and validity (see Section 2) — [Barrett et al. 2019, APS page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/emotional-expressions-reconsidered-challenges-to-inferring-emotion-from-human-facial-movements.html).

**Background knowledge, NOT verified in this session (check against Barrett et al. 2019, Fig. 4/Table 1, Ekman & Friesen EMFACS, and Turrisi et al. 2026 before use).** Commonly cited Ekman/EMFACS prototype AUs, with the plausible ARKit/MediaPipe blendshape equivalents:

| Emotion label (hypothesized) | Typical prototype AUs | Approximate ARKit/MediaPipe blendshapes |
|---|---|---|
| Happiness (Duchenne smile) | AU6 cheek raiser + AU12 lip corner puller | `cheekSquintL/R` (AU6, often confounded with `eyeSquint`) + `mouthSmileL/R` (AU12) |
| Sadness | AU1 inner brow raiser + AU4 brow lowerer + AU15 lip corner depressor | `browInnerUp` (AU1) + `browDownL/R` (AU4) + `mouthFrownL/R` (AU15) |
| Surprise | AU1 + AU2 outer brow raiser + AU5 upper lid raiser + AU26 jaw drop | `browInnerUp` + `browOuterUpL/R` (AU2) + `eyeWideL/R` (AU5) + `jawOpen` (AU26/27) |
| Fear | AU1+2+4+5+7+20+26 | as above, plus `eyeSquintL/R` (AU7, approx.) and `mouthStretchL/R` (AU20) |
| Anger | AU4 + AU5 + AU7 lid tightener + AU23 lip tightener (sometimes AU24 lip pressor) | `browDownL/R` (AU4) + `eyeWideL/R` (AU5) + `eyeSquintL/R` (AU7 approx.) + `mouthPressL/R` (AU24). **No direct AU23 blendshape** (`mouthRollUpper/Lower` and `mouthPress` are only partial proxies) |
| Disgust | AU9 nose wrinkler and/or AU10 upper lip raiser (+AU15/16/17) | `noseSneerL/R` (AU9) + `mouthUpperUpL/R` (AU10) (+`mouthLowerDownL/R` AU16, `mouthShrugLower` AU17) |
| Contempt | Unilateral AU12 and/or AU14 dimpler (R/L) | Asymmetry in `mouthDimpleL` vs `mouthDimpleR` (AU14) and/or `mouthSmileL` vs `mouthSmileR` |
| Frustration | **No FACS/EMFACS prototype exists.** It is not one of the "basic emotions" | No mapping can be justified (see Gaps) |

Also background knowledge, unverified: the `eyeLook*` blendshapes (`eyeLookUpLeft` and so on) encode gaze direction, not facial muscle AUs (FACS treats eye position as codes 61 to 64). An "eye roll" would have to be inferred from gaze trajectory plus head pose over time, not from a single AU. MediaPipe's 52 categories are believed to be `_neutral` plus 51 ARKit names (ARKit's `tongueOut` is absent). Confirm this against the model card.

### Inferences
- Steady can say truthfully that it measures facial movements (for example "brow lowering", "lip pressing", "nose wrinkling", "one-sided mouth movement"). Calling these "AU-like" is defensible with the Turrisi et al. 2026 mapping as a citation. Calling them FACS AU intensities is not, because the mapping is expert consensus without a reported validation against FACS-certified coding.
- Because AU23 (the core "tight lips" muscle in the anger prototype) has no clean blendshape, any "anger/tension" composite built from `mouthPress` + `browDown` departs from even the basic-emotion-theory prototype.
- Mirroring bugs can silently swap left and right, which would corrupt any unilateral signal such as a contempt-like asymmetry. Steady should feed MediaPipe un-mirrored frames.
- A "frustration" score has no anatomical or FACS basis to cite, so it would be a product invention.

### Gaps
- Full text of Barrett et al. 2019, Turrisi et al. 2026 and the Ozel cheat sheet could not be retrieved, so the AU table above is unverified background knowledge.
- No source found reporting per-blendshape accuracy of MediaPipe against FACS-coded ground truth (for example on DISFA or BP4D), or its accuracy by skin tone, age or head pose.
- No reliable source found for frustration-specific AUs. Some affective-computing work on learning contexts reportedly links frustration with AU4 or AU14, but it was not retrieved here.

## 2. Basic emotion theory versus constructionist and behavioral-ecology views; what Barrett et al. (2019) concluded; counterarguments and evidence from 2019 to 2026

### Takeaway
The 2019 PSPI review concluded that facial configurations are not reliable or specific enough across contexts, people and cultures to serve as "diagnostic displays" of emotion. For example, people scowl when angry only about a quarter of the time, and they scowl for many other reasons. Defenders of basic emotion theory point to large-scale data showing that expression–context associations are largely shared across cultures (Cowen & Keltner 2021). That data, however, concerns perceived expression categories in context, not verified inner feelings. The field still disagrees about universality. There is broad agreement that context strongly shapes what a face means and that reading feelings off a face without context is unreliable.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak (2019), "Emotional Expressions Reconsidered: Challenges to Inferring Emotion From Human Facial Movements," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 20(1):1–68 — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/emotional-expressions-reconsidered-challenges-to-inferring-emotion-from-human-facial-movements.html); [PDF mirror](https://affective-science.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/barrett-et-al-2019-emotional-expressions-reconsidered-challenges-to-inferring-emotion-from-human-facial-movements.pdf); [Caltech record](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/yt8mq-1vg37).
- **Core conclusion:** Facial movements are not reliable and specific enough across contexts, individuals and cultures to serve as diagnostic displays of any emotional state — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/emotional-expressions-reconsidered-challenges-to-inferring-emotion-from-human-facial-movements.html) (snippet-verified).
- **Four criteria:** A facial configuration can justify inferences about emotion only if it meets reliability, specificity, generalizability and validity. Reliability means a scowl occurs whenever someone is angry. Specificity means a scowl rarely occurs when someone is not angry — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/emotional-expressions-reconsidered-challenges-to-inferring-emotion-from-human-facial-movements.html) (snippet-verified).
- **Evidence thresholds:** Their Table 4, adapted from Haidt & Keltner (1999), counts 70–90% as strong evidence and 20–40% as weak. The authors rated production evidence for reliability and specificity as weak in both lab and naturalistic adult studies — [PMC Table 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6640856/table/T4) (snippet-verified; page not fetchable).
- **The 25% figure:** The authors' institutional press release says people scowl when angry about 25% of the time on average, so a scowl is neither necessary nor sufficient for anger. Smiles can signal things other than happiness, such as submission — [Waisman Center press release, 2019](https://www.waisman.wisc.edu/2019/07/24/emotion-detection-applications-built-on-outdated-science-report-warns/); [Waisman Child Emotion Lab](https://childemotion.waisman.wisc.edu/2019/07/22/weaknesses-in-emotion-expression-research-outlined-in-new-report/) (press-release figure; snippet-verified).
- **Terminology:** The authors recommend neutral terms such as "facial configuration" instead of "emotional expression". The press release is headlined as a warning that emotion-detection applications are "built on outdated science" — [Waisman press release](https://www.waisman.wisc.edu/2019/07/24/emotion-detection-applications-built-on-outdated-science-report-warns/) (snippet-verified).
- **Constructionist follow-up (2021):** Le Mau et al., "Professional actors demonstrate variability, not stereotypical expressions, when portraying emotional states in photographs," *Nature Communications* 12:5037. They analyzed 604 photographs of actors given emotion-evoking scenarios written without emotion words. Unsupervised and supervised machine learning found variable configurations, and only fear, happiness and surprise were portrayed with even moderate reliability and specificity. Ratings of the photographs changed when the scenario was shown alongside them, so emotion inferences are context-sensitive. Limitations: static photos and a limited range of cultures — [Nature Communications](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41467-021-25352-6); [Northeastern news](https://news.northeastern.edu/2021/08/20/you-cant-determine-emotion-from-someones-facial-expression-and-neither-can-ai/1) (snippet-verified).
- **Behavioral ecology view (Crivelli & Fridlund 2018, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, "Facial Displays Are Tools for Social Influence"):**
  - Facial displays are social tools that act as lead signs of contingent action in social negotiation, not readouts of feelings. Fridlund: "Our faces are not about us, but about where we want a social interaction to go."
  - The authors cite the "gasping face" being used as a threat display in some small-scale societies.
  - They cite Trobriand Islanders as an exception to the smile–happiness association.
  - They argue that people are never "psychologically alone", so even solitary expressions are social.
  
  Sources: [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/radical-new-theory-proposes-facial-expressions-are-not-emotional-displays-tools); [UCSB news](https://news.ucsb.edu/2018/018839/strategic-expression); [Emotion Researcher: BECV 25 years later](https://emotionresearcher.com/the-behavioral-ecology-view-of-facial-displays-25-years-later/) [older, 2018] (snippet-verified; no independent replications of the gasping-face or Trobriand findings were found).
- **Main counter-evidence (2021):** Cowen, Keltner et al., "Sixteen facial expressions occur in similar contexts worldwide," *Nature* 589:251–257. A deep neural network classified 16 expression types in about 6 million YouTube videos from 144 countries. About 70% of the context–expression associations found in each region were shared across the modern world, which the authors read as support for universality — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-3037-7); [Google Research blog](https://blog.research.google/2021/05/understanding-contextual-facial.html); [Berkeley news](https://news.berkeley.edu/?p=87888) (snippet-verified).
- **Recent reviews (2024–2025):** These remain contested. Mattioli & Cabitza (2024, *Machine Learning and Knowledge Extraction*) review FER debates on cultural/social bias, ethics and reliability. A 2025 *Brain Sciences* conceptual review argues that "Emotional AI may thus be learning not what humans feel, but what they believe they should express" — [Springer AI Review 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10462-025-11271-1); [PMC 2025 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12468198/) (snippet-verified; exact mapping of these URLs to these papers not confirmed because pages were not fetched).

### Inferences
- Cowen et al.'s classifier was trained on human judgments of expression categories. Its results therefore show that perceived expression categories co-occur with similar contexts across cultures, not that a given face reliably indicates a felt emotion. For Steady, both camps support the same practical line: a face can say how a movement is likely to be read in context, but it is weak evidence of what the person feels.
- Even on the most favorable reading (Le Mau's moderate results for fear, happiness and surprise), anger, disgust, sadness and contempt are among the categories with the weakest production evidence. Those are exactly the categories a "meeting coaching" app would be tempted to flag.
- The behavioral-ecology framing ("faces as social signals about where an interaction is going") is a scientifically defensible basis for feedback such as "this may come across as disagreement or disengagement", as opposed to "you are angry".

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the quantitative meta-analysis by Durán & Fernández-Dols (pooled co-occurrence of emotion and predicted expression) or formal basic-emotion-theory rebuttals to the 2019 PSPI paper (for example Keltner, Sauter, Tracy & Cowen 2019 in the *Journal of Nonverbal Behavior*, or Ekman Group responses), so they are not cited here.
- No published methodological critique of Cowen et al. 2021 was surfaced in the searches.
- No 2025–2026 large naturalistic production study (felt emotion against coded face) was found.

## 3. Microexpressions: definition, duration, frequency, detection, METT training, deception detection, and the SPOT critique

### Takeaway
Microexpressions do occur, but they are rare: about 2% of expressions in the main high-stakes coding study. They occur at similar rates in honest and deceptive people and are often partial. The best controlled test of the commercial Micro Expression Training Tool (METT) found it did not improve lie detection over bogus training or no training, with accuracy slightly below chance. A GAO audit found no valid evidence that the behavioral indicators used by the TSA's SPOT program identify threats, and TSA ended the program in FY2017. "Microexpressions reveal hidden emotions or lies" is overstated. "Brief facial movements exist and recognition of them can be trained on a test" is supported.

### Cited Findings
- **Frequency [older, 2008]:** Porter & ten Brinke (2008) coded 700 high-stakes genuine and falsified emotional expressions and found only about 2% were microexpressions. A later analysis of televised missing-child appeals found microexpressions were rare and occurred about equally in genuine and deceptive appeals. Reviews conclude that microexpressions "were not necessarily indicative of deception" and that their rarity limits their use as cues to deceit — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6158306); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3296074/) (snippet-verified; full articles not fetched).
- **People versus expressions:** One figure says about 22% of participants displayed a microexpression at least once, while the 2% figure is per expression. These are different denominators — [Burgoon 2018, Frontiers in Psychology opinion](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01672) (snippet-verified).
- **Burgoon (2018):** In an opinion article in *Frontiers in Psychology* ("Microexpressions are not the best way to catch a liar"), Burgoon argues that false negatives are commonplace, meaning most liars show no microexpressions — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01672) [older, 2018; opinion, not a meta-analysis].
- **METT and lie detection (2019):** Jordan et al. (2019) found the METT group did not outperform bogus-training and no-training groups at detecting lies, and overall accuracy was slightly below chance — [Jordan et al. 2019 PDF (Williams College host)](https://web.williams.edu/Psychology/Faculty/Kassin/files/Jordan%20et%20al%20(2019)%20-%20METT.pdf) (snippet-verified; the host was unreachable for a full read, and journal venue not confirmed here).
- **METT and emotion recognition:** Other METT studies, including one with retail employees, reported better recognition of brief expressions. Those studies measured recognition, not lie detection. Warren et al. (2009) noted there had been "no valid study of the METT" for deception detection — [search summary of METT literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6158306) [older] (snippet-verified).
- **SPOT, 2013 audit:** GAO-14-159 (November 2013) found the available evidence did not support whether the behavioral indicators used in TSA's SPOT program identify threats. GAO found that DHS's April 2011 validation study had methodological weaknesses. It also said decades of peer-reviewed research on detecting deception through human observation "called into question the scientific basis" of TSA's behavior detection. Behavior detection officers and TSA officials agreed some indicators were subjective. GAO recommended limiting future funding until valid evidence existed, and DHS did not concur — [GAO-14-159](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-14-159); [accessible text](https://www.gao.gov/assets/660/658983.txt) [older, 2013].
- **SPOT, 2010 audit:** GAO-10-763 found TSA deployed SPOT without first validating its scientific basis — [GAO-10-763](https://gao.gov/products/GAO-10-763) [older, 2010].
- **SPOT outcome:** In FY2017 TSA ended its behavior detection program and moved behavior detection officers into the general screening workforce. Advocacy summaries (EPIC; House Homeland Security Democrats) used stronger language, such as "no scientifically valid evidence", than GAO's own wording — [GAO-14-159 search summary](https://www.gao.gov/products/GAO-14-159); [EPIC](https://epic.org/2013/11/government-audit-finds-tsas-be.html); [House Homeland Security Democrats](https://democrats-homeland.house.gov/news/reports/tsa-behavior-detection-program-should-be-defunded).

### Inferences
- **Solidly supported:** brief facial movements occur; they are rare; they are not specific to lying; untrained lie detection from behavior is near chance; METT-style training does not improve lie detection in a controlled test; and a US government audit found no validated basis for behavior-based threat detection.
- **Overstated:** that microexpressions reliably "leak" concealed emotions; that spotting them reveals deception; that training makes someone a better lie detector.
- **For Steady:** at typical webcam rates of about 15–30 fps, a 40–500 ms event lasts only about 1–15 frames. MediaPipe blendshapes on webcam video are noisy at that timescale, so "microexpression detection" would be both scientifically and technically weak. Steady should not claim to detect microexpressions or "hidden"/"true" emotions.

### Gaps
- The canonical duration range (about 1/25 s to 1/2 s, roughly 40–500 ms) comes from the assignment brief and common usage. No primary source for it was retrieved in this session (candidates are Ekman's work and Yan et al. 2013 on micro-expression duration).
- The full Porter & ten Brinke 2008 paper could not be read to confirm whether most microexpressions were partial (upper or lower face only).
- No 2020–2026 meta-analysis on microexpression detection accuracy or METT was found.

## 4. Cultural, individual, clinical and measurement variation

### Takeaway
Who is being measured and how strongly affects facial signals. Display rules and culture shift what faces do (Section 2). Autistic people produce facial expressions less often and more briefly (moderate effect). Parkinson's hypomimia reduces smiling and causes observers to judge people more negatively. Automated AU detection degrades with head turn beyond about 15 degrees and with spontaneous, non-posed expressions. Any per-user signal therefore needs a personal baseline and must never be read as evidence of feeling.

### Cited Findings
- **Autism:** A meta-analysis (Trevisan et al. 2018, *Autism Research*; 67 effect sizes) found autistic participants produced facial expressions less often and for shorter durations, summary effect -0.481 (moderate). Intensity and reaction-time differences were largely absent. Differences were larger for spontaneous than for prompted expressions and varied with age, intellectual functioning and measurement method — [PubMed 30393953](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30393953/) [older, 2018] (snippet-verified). Autistic people's expressions are also sometimes rated as ambiguous by observers — [The Transmitter/Spectrum](https://www.thetransmitter.org/spectrum/people-autism-sometimes-give-ambiguous-looks/).
- **Parkinson's disease, reduced expression:** Hypomimia (reduced facial expression, the "masked face") is common in PD and is linked to motor symptoms and altered emotion processing — [2024 narrative review, "The Story behind the Mask"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10814039/). Smiling frequency and strength are reduced. Non-depressed patients showed reduced amplitude and delayed onset of zygomaticus (smile) activity in response to happy stimuli — [Frontiers in Psychology 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00780/text) [older] (snippet-verified).
- **Parkinson's disease, how others see it:** Observer studies show people with moderate facial masking are perceived more negatively than those with minimal masking, and the bias is stronger for women. The proposed mechanism is lower perceived predictability and trustworthiness. Facial masking reduced quality of life through stigma (n=90). Depression is a confound because it lowers both facial expressivity and social wellbeing — [PMC5003752](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5003752); [PMC4894910](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4894910) (snippet-verified).
- **Automated AU detection, spontaneous interaction [older, 2015]:** In a study of spontaneous, unscripted social interaction (Girard et al., *Behavior Research Methods*), differences in AU detection related to gender, ethnicity, pose and average pixel intensity were mostly small. Fewer than 6% of frames could be coded manually but not automatically. Earlier work suggested robustness up to about 15 degrees from frontal, and rapid head movement remains hard to track — [Behavior Research Methods (s13428-014-0536-1)](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13428-014-0536-1); [PMC4461567](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4461567) (snippet-verified).
- **Head turn:** A 2022 viewpoint study (0–45 degrees, static images) found OpenFace performance decreased as the face turned sideways — [search result, PMC8235167 / MDPI](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8235167) (snippet-verified; exact paper-to-URL mapping not confirmed).
- **Tool comparison and domain shift:** A 2021 comparison of FaceReader, OpenFace and AFAR expected higher accuracy for posed than spontaneous expressions and noted OpenFace can confuse AU10 (upper lip raiser) with AU12 (smile). A cross-domain study concluded "caution is warranted when applying AU classifiers from one domain to another" — [search summary of FACS automation literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7202467) (snippet-verified; exact paper-to-URL mapping not confirmed).
- **Vendor claims:** AFFDEX 2.0 claims better tracking under difficult poses and darker illumination, but this is a vendor toolkit paper, not independent validation — [AFFDEX 2.0, arXiv 2202.12059](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2202.12059).
- **Culture:** Crivelli & Fridlund report culture-specific uses of displays, such as the gasping face as a threat in some small-scale societies and Trobrianders not linking smiles with happiness. Cowen et al. 2021 report about 70% cross-regional sharing of expression–context associations, which also implies about 30% regional variation — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/radical-new-theory-proposes-facial-expressions-are-not-emotional-displays-tools); [Nature 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-3037-7).

### Inferences
- **Baselines:** Low expressivity (autism, Parkinson's, depression, facial palsy, Botox, some medications) would make population-normed "engagement"/"warmth" scores systematically low for these users. Asymmetric faces (facial paralysis, Bell's palsy, stroke) would produce false "contempt-like asymmetry" flags. Per-user baselining and a way to opt out of specific signals are needed.
- **Webcam conditions:** Webcams in meetings are often off-axis (laptop below eye level, second monitor), so head pitch and yaw often exceed 15 degrees. Steady should gate blendshape-based feedback on MediaPipe's head-pose matrix and suppress output outside a frontal window.
- Since observers already judge masked or low-expressivity faces negatively, a coaching app that nudges such users to "smile more" could add to that stigma. Product copy should treat it as optional self-presentation advice, not correction.

### Gaps
- **Depression:** no meta-analysis on facial expressivity was retrieved; only its role as a confound in PD studies.
- **Facial paralysis:** no source retrieved on automated measurement for these users (inference above is unverified).
- **Age:** no source retrieved on age effects (wrinkles affecting AU detection, older adults' resting faces).
- **Lighting and skin tone:** no source retrieved quantifying MediaPipe blendshape accuracy specifically.
- **Display rules:** Matsumoto's and Ekman's classic display-rules studies were not retrieved.

## 5. Validity and bias of automated facial expression analysis and "emotion AI"

### Takeaway
Commercial emotion classifiers are much less accurate on spontaneous expressions than on posed ones. In a 2020 test of eight commercial systems, accuracy ranged from 48% to 62%, against about 72% for humans. Systems have shown racial bias, scoring Black faces as angrier at equal smile levels. Because emotion AI's construct validity is contested (Section 2), even a perfect classifier would only be predicting human labels of faces. The EU AI Act separately prohibits emotion inference from biometric data in the workplace, with narrow exceptions. That is directly relevant to a work-meeting product.

### Cited Findings
- **Accuracy of commercial systems:** Dupré et al. (2020, *PLOS ONE*) tested eight commercial classifiers on 937 dynamic videos from two databases. Recognition accuracy ranged from 48% to 62%, against about 72% for human observers. The two best systems approached human performance on posed expressions, but accuracy was consistently lower on spontaneous expressions, though still above chance — [PLOS ONE via PMC7182192](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7182192/); [DCU news](https://www.dcu.ie/news/news/2020/04/new-research-shows-artificial-intelligence-still-lags-behind-humans-when-it-comes) [2020] (snippet-verified).
- **Racial bias:** Rhue (SSRN working paper 2018; later published) analyzed about 400 NBA player portraits (2016–17 season). Face++ rated Black players as angrier than white players even after controlling for smiling, and Black faces were scored as angrier whenever expression was ambiguous. A later audit covered Amazon Rekognition, Face++ and Microsoft — [SSRN 3281765](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3281765); [UMD Smith Brain Trust 2022](https://www.rhsmith.umd.edu/research/race-factor-facial-recognition-tech); [Rhue, via Blavity/The Conversation](https://blavity.com/emotion-reading-tech-fails-the-racial-bias-test) [older, 2018–2019] (snippet-verified; peer-review venue not confirmed).
- **Inflated benchmarks (adjacent evidence):** A 2025 meta-analysis of 51 speech emotion recognition studies (not facial) found most used scripted or acted data, which inflates performance metrics that do not carry over to real-world use. This is the same posed-versus-in-the-wild problem seen in face datasets — [Khalifa University research news](https://www.ku.ac.ae/?p=109269) (snippet-verified; adjacent modality).
- **Contested validity:** A FAccT 2025 systematic review on privacy harms describes emotion AI as contested for its validity, bias and accuracy — [FAccT 2025 paper](https://facctconference.org/static/docs/facct2025-206archivalpdfs/facct2025-final394-acmpaginated.pdf) (snippet-verified). Le Mau et al. (2021) concluded that neither people nor AI can determine emotion from facial movements alone — [Northeastern news](https://news.northeastern.edu/2021/08/20/you-cant-determine-emotion-from-someones-facial-expression-and-neither-can-ai/1).
- **EU AI Act, the prohibition:** Article 5(1)(f) prohibits AI systems that infer the emotions of a natural person in the workplace and education institutions based on biometric data, except for medical or safety reasons — [EU AI Act Service Desk, Article 5](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-5); [FPF analysis](https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-eu-ai-act-unpacking-the-prohibition-of-emotion-recognition-in-the-workplace-and-education-institutions/); [Wolters Kluwer Global Workplace Law blog](https://legalblogs.wolterskluwer.com/global-workplace-law-and-policy/the-prohibition-of-ai-emotion-recognition-technologies-in-the-workplace-under-the-ai-act/).
- **EU AI Act, how the 2025 Commission guidelines read it (per commentary):**
  - The medical/safety exception is to be read narrowly. It does not cover general wellbeing, so workplace burnout or stress detection stays prohibited.
  - Physical states such as pain or fatigue are not emotions under the definition.
  - The prohibition covers emotions but does not mention "intentions". FPF flags inferring an employee's intention to resign from facial expressions in meetings or videocalls as an unsettled case.
  
  Sources: [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-eu-ai-act-unpacking-the-prohibition-of-emotion-recognition-in-the-workplace-and-education-institutions/); [MobiHealthNews](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/eu-issues-guidelines-banned-ai-practices); [Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2025/02/17/understanding-the-eu-ai-acts-prohibited-practices-key-workplace-and-advertising-102k011) (law-firm and think-tank commentary; full guidelines not read).

### Inferences
- Reported FER accuracy, typically on posed benchmarks, will overstate accuracy on a user's own webcam in real meetings. No accuracy figure from posed datasets should appear in Steady's marketing.
- **Legal (for counsel, not a legal opinion):** Steady infers from the user's face during work meetings, so labeling outputs as emotions ("you seemed angry/frustrated/stressed") risks falling inside Article 5(1)(f) for EU users, even if only the user sees the output. Reporting observable facial movements without inferring emotion is the lower-risk framing.
- Background knowledge, not retrieved in this session: AI Act Recital 18 states that emotion recognition does not include "mere detection of readily apparent expressions, gestures or movements" such as a frown or smile "unless they are used for identifying or inferring emotions." If confirmed, this supports a "movement, not emotion" design. Counsel should verify the text and whether a self-coaching tool used at work is "in the area of the workplace".

### Gaps
- No independent benchmark of MediaPipe blendshape accuracy or demographic bias was found. Rhue's work concerns commercial emotion APIs on static portraits, not MediaPipe.
- No 2024–2026 head-to-head study of facial emotion AI accuracy on in-the-wild workplace video was found. The 2024–2025 literature retrieved is mostly conceptual or ethical.
- No GDPR biometric-data analysis or US state-law analysis (for example Illinois BIPA) was done here; that is out of scope for this note.

## 6. Which facial signals most reliably shape how others perceive a person, as distinct from what the person feels

### Takeaway
The evidence on how faces are perceived is stronger than the evidence on what they reveal about feeling. Observers quickly and consistently draw trait impressions from faces. Faces (even resting, neutral ones) that resemble anger, such as lowered brows and a downturned mouth, are judged less trustworthy and more hostile and dominant. Reduced expressivity is judged negatively. Contempt signals (eye rolls, one-sided mouth movements) are treated as damaging in relationship research. These impressions are often inaccurate about the person but still affect social outcomes. Coaching that targets "how this may come across" therefore rests on firmer ground than claims about inner states, though no workplace-meeting-specific evidence was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Resting faces:** Neutral faces that structurally resemble anger (downturned brows) are judged less trustworthy, less likeable, and more dominant, hostile and threatening. Oosterhof and Todorov describe this as an "overgeneralization" effect. Changing a face's trustworthiness from high to low increased perceived anger intensity and decreased perceived happiness — [PMC4557906](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4557906); [PMC3778026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3778026); [Princeton: Structural resemblance to emotional expressions predicts evaluation of emotionally neutral faces](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/structural-resemblance-to-emotional-expressions-predicts-evaluati/) [older, 2008–2015] (snippet-verified).
- **Accuracy versus consequences:** These trait impressions "are not necessarily accurate", but "they affect important social outcomes" — [PMC3778026 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3778026) (snippet-verified).
- **Low expressivity:** People with moderate Parkinsonian facial masking are perceived more negatively (less predictable, less trustworthy), more so for women — [PMC5003752](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5003752) (snippet-verified).
- **Contempt and eye rolls:** The Gottman Institute lists contempt as one of four conflict behaviors linked to marital instability and describes eye rolls and chuckles as contemptuous gestures partners often don't notice in themselves — [Gottman Institute blog](https://www.gottman.com/blog/what-causes-contempt-in-relationships). Popular claims that eye-rolling is "the top predictor of divorce", or that specific accuracy figures (91%, 93.6%) apply, come from secondary media and were not verified against peer-reviewed sources — [HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/this-behavior-is-the-top-predictor-of-divorce_l_6110d071e4b0ed63e657f502) (treat as unverified).
- **Context shapes perception:** In Le Mau et al. (2021), observers' emotion ratings of the same face changed when the scenario was shown alongside it — [Nature Communications 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41467-021-25352-6) (snippet-verified).
- **Displays as social signals:** Under the behavioral ecology view, displays signal social intent and the likely next action ("where we want a social interaction to go"), which fits a perception-focused framing — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/radical-new-theory-proposes-facial-expressions-are-not-emotional-displays-tools).
- **Perceivers' biases, including machine perceivers:** Automated systems rated Black faces as angrier at equal smiling. This suggests "perceived anger" labels can carry demographic bias, whether the perceiver is a human-trained model or a person — [SSRN, Rhue](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3281765).

### Inferences
- **Most defensible signals to surface as "how you may come across"** (movement-level, not emotion-level, and with per-user baselines):
  - sustained brow lowering (`browDown*`), the "scowl" component associated with perceived anger, hostility and low trustworthiness;
  - sustained lip pressing or rolling (`mouthPress*`, `mouthRoll*`) as "tight lips";
  - one-sided mouth movement (asymmetry in `mouthSmile*`/`mouthDimple*`), the smirk associated with perceived contempt;
  - nose wrinkle or upper-lip raise (`noseSneer*`, `mouthUpperUp*`), perceived as disgust;
  - an upward gaze sweep (`eyeLookUp*` plus head pose), a possible eye roll;
  - low overall expressivity or few smiles, perceived as disengagement or coldness.
  
  Each should be framed as "others may read this as...", never "you felt...".
- **Claims Steady should avoid:** detecting emotions (anger, contempt, frustration, stress, burnout); detecting microexpressions or "true/hidden" feelings; detecting lying; any accuracy figure from posed datasets; and any implied link to clinical states.
- **Safeguards:** Because resting-face structure alone triggers trait impressions, Steady should separate changes from the user's own baseline from static facial structure, and should not imply that a user's natural face is a problem.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that quantifies how specific facial movements (brow lowering, lip pressing, eye rolls, smirks) in video meetings affect colleagues' judgments of competence, warmth or hostility.
- Gottman's primary peer-reviewed facial-coding studies on contempt were not retrieved, only the institute's blog.
- No evidence was found on whether people perceive eye rolls in video meetings reliably, or on how webcam angle changes perceived expression.
- No source was found on whether feedback about one's own facial movements improves how others perceive one (coaching efficacy). That is a key untested assumption for Steady.
