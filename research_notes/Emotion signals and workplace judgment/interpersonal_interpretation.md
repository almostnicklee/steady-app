## 1. Emotions as social information: the EASI model and how displayed anger, frustration, contempt, anxiety and warmth change other people's behavior

### Takeaway
Under Van Kleef's EASI model, a displayed emotion works as information about the sender's goals, limits and intentions. Observers respond in two ways: they draw inferences ("they're near their limit, I should concede") and they react affectively ("I feel hostile, I'll retaliate"). Anger can win concessions in the short run. It does so only under specific conditions: the anger is aimed at the offer and not the person, the observer is dependent or low-power, and the dispute concerns interests and not values. Even then it erodes trust and can provoke covert retaliation. Evidence for warmth and anxiety is thinner in what I retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- **Core model (older, 2008-2010):** EASI holds that emotions shape the behavior of people who observe them, mainly by supplying information about the situation. Observers read an opponent's emotion as a signal of that opponent's limits and goals. — [Van Kleef et al., EASI model chapter (UvA)](https://dare.uva.nl/document/2/61635); [ResearchGate copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/280557074_Emotion_in_conflict_and_negotiation_Introducing_the_emotions_as_social_information_EASI_model); [Van Kleef 2010, Social & Personality Psychology Compass preprint](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/1479335/90694_Van_Kleef_2010_SPPC_PrePrint_Emotion_as_Social_Information.pdf)
- **Anger leads to concessions (older, mid-2000s experiments):** Negotiators facing an angry opponent conceded more than those facing a happy one. They used the opponent's emotion to estimate his or her limits and then made counter-offers based on that estimate. — [Van Kleef 2009, "How Emotions Regulate Social Life," Current Directions in Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-8721.2009.01633.x); [EASI chapter](https://dare.uva.nl/document/2/61635)
- **Boundary conditions:** Emotions aimed at a negotiator's *offer* carry relatively more information than emotions aimed at the negotiator *as a person*. Anger drew concessions when observers were in a vulnerable position (for example, an unfavorable reputation). Observers' motivation to process information also moderates the effect. — [EASI chapter (UvA)](https://dare.uva.nl/document/2/61635); [Van Kleef 2009](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-8721.2009.01633.x)
- **Interests vs. values:** One review reports that anger pays off only when the conflict is about interests. In disputes about values it can trigger retaliation. — [Negotiation Journal review "State of the Art With Feeling: How Emotions Shape Negotiation" (vol. 30, no. 4)](https://direct.mit.edu/ngtn/article-pdf/30/4/455/2379468/nejo12071.pdf)
- **Meta-analysis (2020, the most recent synthesis I found):** Sharma, Elfenbein and colleagues pooled 64 published and unpublished studies spanning three decades. Displaying negative emotions tends to draw concessions and helps the sender claim individual value, but it *diminishes trust* and other subjective outcomes. Power, culture, emotion regulation, and design or negotiation phase moderate the effects. Mediators are the observer's inferences about the sender's limits and toughness, plus affective reactions. Effect sizes were not visible in the snippets. — [WashU profile, Human Performance 2020](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-effects-of-emotional-expressions-in-negotiation-a-meta-analys/); [DOI 10.1080/08959285.2020.1783667](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/08959285.2020.1783667)
- **Hidden cost of anger:** Wang, Northcraft and Van Kleef conclude that the value-claiming advantage of expressed anger has to be weighed against the cost of eliciting covert retaliation. — [ANU research portal, "Beyond negotiated outcomes: The hidden costs of anger expression in dyadic negotiation"](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/beyond-negotiated-outcomes-the-hidden-costs-of-anger-expression-i/)
- **Anger is read through the sender's gender and race (details in Q4):** Observers attribute angry women's and angry Black women's displays to internal traits, which lowers the status, performance and leadership ratings they receive. — [Brescoll & Uhlmann 2008, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18315800/); [Motro et al. 2022 summary](https://www.ethicalpsychology.com/2022/04/race-and-reactions-to-womens.html)
- **Sarcasm (a contempt-adjacent signal):** Across studies, people who expressed or received sarcasm reported *more conflict*, and also showed more creativity, which was mediated by abstract thinking. When sarcasm passed between people who trusted each other, creativity still rose but conflict did not. — [Huang, Gino & Galinsky 2015, OBHDP 131:162-177 (PDF)](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/55dcde36e4b0df55a96ab220/t/569e634a05f8e2b24db420a4/1453220682892/Huang+Gino+Galinsky+OBHDP+2015.pdf); [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v131y2015icp162-177.html)

### Inferences
- For a meeting coach, the anger evidence is double-edged. Anger can win the point, but the same display lowers trust, invites covert retaliation, and costs women and Black women more. That gives a nudge good grounds to flag anger aimed at a *person* (which carries less information and does more relational damage) more strongly than frustration aimed at an *issue*.
- Most meetings are ongoing relationships, not one-shot negotiations, so the trust and retaliation costs probably outweigh any concession gains in typical workplace use. This is my extrapolation; the negotiation studies are mostly one-shot lab tasks.
- The sarcasm results suggest that relationship context, especially existing trust, changes how a signal lands. A coach without that context may over-flag banter between close colleagues.

### Gaps
- I retrieved no primary evidence on how displayed *anxiety*, *warmth/happiness* or *contempt* specifically shift concessions or cooperation within the EASI line (for example, the work on disappointment, guilt or regret as supplication emotions). The snippets cover anger only.
- Effect sizes from the Sharma et al. 2020 meta-analysis were not visible.
- I found no studies of EASI-type effects in *video-mediated* negotiations or meetings.

## 2. Emotional contagion and mimicry, escalation and negative reciprocity, first impressions and thin slices, and negativity bias

### Takeaway
People automatically mimic others' expressions and then converge on their feelings (Hatfield). In work groups, a single person's mood spreads and changes cooperation and conflict (Barsade 2002). Strangers form impressions from very brief exposures, under 30 seconds of silent video or about 100 ms of a face, and those impressions predict later evaluations. Negative information carries more weight, forms faster, and resists disconfirmation more than positive information. Negativity bias is robust but not universal, and the often-quoted "5:1" ratio comes from popular sources.

### Cited Findings
- **Contagion via mimicry (older, 1993-1994):** Hatfield, Cacioppo and Rapson describe emotional contagion as a largely automatic tendency to mimic others' expressions, voice and posture and, through that mimicry, to converge emotionally. Their book proposes that moment-to-moment emotional experience is shaped by feedback from that mimicry. — [Hatfield, Cacioppo & Rapson 1993, Current Directions 2(3), APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/1467-8721.ep10770953/); [Cambridge book chapter on facial, vocal and postural feedback](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/emotional-contagion/mechanisms-of-emotional-contagion-ii-emotional-experience-and-facial-vocal-and-postural-feedback/C6D9136202CFCE036C5E266048EC9C33)
- **Words vs. faces (secondary summary only):** People's *conscious judgments* of others' feelings are reportedly shaped mainly by what those others say, while their *own emotions* are more affected by the others' nonverbal expressions. — [Wikipedia, Emotional contagion (secondary)](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Affective_contagion)
- **Group contagion at work (older, 2002):** In a lab study of managerial decision groups, a trained confederate displayed moods that spread to the other members. Positive contagion produced more cooperation, less conflict, and higher perceived task performance; the working-paper version reports the opposite for negative contagion. — [Barsade 2002, "The Ripple Effect," Administrative Science Quarterly 47(4):644-675 (PDF)](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/2002/barsade2002a.pdf); [Penn repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/mgmt_papers/72); [2001 working paper](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/2001/barsade2001ua.pdf)
- **Thin slices (older, 1993):** Strangers' ratings of teachers from silent clips under 30 seconds significantly predicted end-of-semester student evaluations. In a follow-up, 6- and 15-second clips still related strongly to a principal's ratings. Sources disagree on exact clip lengths (one textbook says 3 x 10 s; Wikipedia says 2, 5 and 10 s). — [Ambady & Rosenthal 1993 "Half a Minute," JPSP 64(3):431-441 (PDF hosted by Stony Brook)](https://www.math.stonybrook.edu/~bishop/classes/math598.F25/Half_a_Minute.pdf); [Open textbook summary](https://opentextbc.ca/socialpsychology/?p=3232)
- **Face impressions in about 100 ms (older, 2006; secondary only):** A tenth of a second of exposure to a face produced trait judgments that correlated highly with judgments made by people given unlimited time. In Todorov's related work, the candidate rated as having the more competent-looking face won about 68% of races. — [Lumen social psychology textbook, Initial Impression Formation](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-social-psychology/chapter/initial-impression-formation/)
- **Negativity bias (older, 2001):** In impression formation, learning something bad about a new acquaintance carries more weight than learning something good. Bad impressions and bad stereotypes form faster and resist disconfirmation more than good ones, and bad information is processed more thoroughly. — [Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Finkenauer & Vohs 2001, Review of General Psychology (PDF)](https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Baumeister_2001.pdf); [SAGE record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1207/S15327957PSPR0504_2)
- **Qualifiers on negativity bias:** Rozin & Royzman argue that negativity bias is often striking but "far from universal." A philosophical critique argues that its implications are blunted in domains where good outnumbers bad. — [Rozin & Royzman 2001 (PDF)](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/2001/rozin2001a.pdf); [Review of Philosophy and Psychology critique](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs13164-018-0382-7)
- **"5 positive to 1 negative" ratio (popular source only):** Couples who keep at least five positive interactions for every negative one during conflict are described as more resilient. This comes from couples research, not workplace research, and I saw it only in secondary coverage. — [Psychology Today, "Four ways to ruin and repair your relationship"](https://psychologytoday.com/us/blog/intersections/202009/four-ways-ruin-and-repair-your-relationship)
- **The opening shapes the trajectory (older, 1999):** In 124 newlywed couples, coding just the first 3 minutes of a conflict discussion predicted marital outcome over 6 years. Couples who later divorced started with more negative and less positive emotion. The divorced subgroup was small (about 17 couples). — [Carrère & Gottman 1999, Family Process 38(3):293-301, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10526767/); [UW news release](https://washington.edu/news/1999/09/27/first-three-minutes-of-discussion-about-on-going-area-of-marital-conflict-are-predictive-of-divorce-for-newlyweds)

### Inferences
- Thin-slice and first-3-minutes findings together imply that the *opening* of a meeting or a hard conversation carries disproportionate weight. A coach may add the most value in the first minutes.
- Under negativity bias, one contempt or anger signal plausibly outweighs several warm ones. Asymmetric thresholds, with stronger warnings for negative signals than prompts for positive ones, are consistent with this. The exact ratio for workplaces has not been established.
- Contagion runs through mimicry of nonverbal channels, so a user's tense face or voice can change how others *feel* even when their words are fine. A coach that watches face and voice, not only words, makes sense on these grounds.

### Gaps
- I found no primary sources on escalation spirals or negative reciprocity in conflict (for example, sequential-analysis work showing that negative affect is reciprocated more reliably than positive affect). Gottman's reciprocity findings appear only in popular sources.
- The Willis & Todorov (2006) primary paper was not retrieved, only textbook summaries.
- I found no workplace-specific replication of the 5:1 ratio. Treat it as a couples heuristic.
- No 2020s replication data on Barsade-style contagion in video meetings was found.

## 3. Behaviors that most damage a conversation: contempt signals, interrupting, raised voice, sarcasm, defensiveness, stonewalling, dominating talk time

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence on conversation-damaging behaviors comes from Gottman's couples research, which names criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling as the "Four Horsemen", with contempt the strongest predictor of breakdown. Most of the accessible material on it is popular or institute-authored, and its prediction-accuracy figures are disputed. Woolley's collective-intelligence work links uneven turn-taking (one person dominating) to worse group performance. Sarcasm raises conflict unless there is trust. I found no solid sourced evidence on interruptions, raised voice, or eye rolls in workplace meetings specifically.

### Cited Findings
- **The Four Horsemen:** Criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling are the four communication patterns Gottman identifies as most destructive. — [Gottman Institute research page](https://www.gottman.com/research/); [TIME](https://time.com/3629761/fix-relationship-problems/)
- **Contempt is the strongest predictor (popular source):** Contempt is called the single best predictor of divorce because it conveys moral superiority. One report says a partner's contempt predicted the number of infectious illnesses the recipient had over the next four years. — [Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/us/blog/intersections/202009/four-ways-ruin-and-repair-your-relationship)
- **Harsh startup:** The Gottman Institute defines "harsh startup" as opening a discussion with criticism or sarcasm, which it treats as a form of contempt, and says such discussions tend to end negatively. — [Gottman Institute blog on Carrère & Gottman 1999](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-research-predicting-divorce-among-newlyweds-from-the-first-three-minutes-of-a-marital-conflict-discussion/)
- **Accuracy claims are contested:** The Gottman site claims prediction accuracy averaging over 90%; secondary sources cite 93.6% (1994, with Levenson) or "96%". These figures come from institute or marketing material, not peer-reviewed abstracts, and should be treated cautiously. — [Gottman research page](https://www.gottman.com/research/); [Gottman blog](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-research-predicting-divorce-among-newlyweds-from-the-first-three-minutes-of-a-marital-conflict-discussion/)
- **Dominating talk time (older, 2010):** Groups have a general "collective intelligence" factor, c, which correlates with members' average social sensitivity and the *equality of conversational turn-taking*, not with average or maximum individual IQ. Groups where one person dominated were less collectively intelligent. The proportion of women also predicted c, probably through social sensitivity. — [Woolley et al. 2010, Science, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20929725/); [Harvard Gender Action Portal summary](https://www.gap.hks.harvard.edu/evidence-collective-intelligence-factor-performance-human-groups)
- **Sarcasm:** It raises reported conflict for both the person expressing it and the person receiving it; trust removes the conflict cost. — [Huang, Gino & Galinsky 2015](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/55dcde36e4b0df55a96ab220/t/569e634a05f8e2b24db420a4/1453220682892/Huang+Gino+Galinsky+OBHDP+2015.pdf); [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2015/07/go-ahead-be-sarcastic)
- **Anger aimed at the person, not the issue:** It carries less information and risks retaliation (see Q1). — [EASI chapter](https://dare.uva.nl/document/2/61635); [Wang, Northcraft & Van Kleef](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/beyond-negotiated-outcomes-the-hidden-costs-of-anger-expression-i/)

### Inferences
- The best-supported targets for detection are (a) contempt or sarcasm markers, (b) person-directed hostility, and (c) talk-time imbalance. Talk-time share is also the easiest to measure objectively on-device.
- The Gottman constructs come from *romantic couples*. Carrying them over to coworkers is plausible but not established in what I retrieved. A product team should present them as analogies, not workplace-validated rules.
- Defensiveness and stonewalling (withdrawal, going silent, camera off, minimal responses) are harder to detect from the speaker's own feed, and they overlap with neutral behaviors such as listening and latency pauses (see Q5).

### Gaps
- **Interruptions:** I found no sourced workplace studies on interruption effects (perceived dominance or rudeness, gender asymmetries). The search returned nothing usable.
- **Raised voice / vocal intensity:** I found no sourced studies.
- **Eye rolls and smirks at work:** No workplace-specific primary studies were retrieved. The contempt evidence is from couples.
- **Stonewalling physiology:** Gottman's "flooding" work, including heart-rate thresholds, was not retrieved.
- **Woolley c factor:** Its replication status is not verified here. I'm aware from background knowledge that there are later critiques and a supportive meta-analysis, but I did not source them in this session, so the report writer should not state either.

## 4. Perception errors: misread neutral faces, attribution error, illusion of transparency, spotlight effect, and how culture, gender, race and neurodivergence change how the same behavior is read

### Takeaway
Speakers overestimate how visible their feelings are (illusion of transparency) and how much others notice them (spotlight effect). Observers, for their part, read faces unreliably: facial configurations are not consistent, specific signals of emotion across contexts, people and cultures (Barrett et al. 2019). The *same* behavior is read differently depending on who shows it. Angry women and angry Black women are given internal, trait-based attributions and lower evaluations, though one conceptual replication found the opposite. Observers higher in implicit prejudice see anger more readily in Black faces. Non-autistic observers form negative thin-slice impressions of autistic people based on style, not content.

### Cited Findings
- **Illusion of transparency (older, 1998):** Gilovich, Savitsky and Medvec showed that people overestimate how well others can read their internal states. In the deception paradigm, according to a secondary summary, about half of liars thought they had been detected while only about a quarter actually were. — [Kellogg/Northwestern record, JPSP 1998](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1998/the-illusion-of-transparency-biased-assessments-of-others-ability/?p=1); [Wikipedia (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_transparency)
- **Intervention (older, 2003; secondary):** In Savitsky & Gilovich (2003), speakers told about the illusion of transparency reported less stress, and their speeches were rated better. — [Wikipedia (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_transparency)
- **Mixed replications:** A Minnesota State master's thesis did not reproduce the effect. MacInnis, Mackinnon and MacIntyre suggested that the public-speaking version may reflect a social script that frames speech anxiety as normal. — [MSU Mankato thesis](https://cornerstone.lib.mnsu.edu/etds/27); [Wikipedia (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_transparency)
- **Spotlight effect (older, 2000):** Students wearing an embarrassing (Barry Manilow) T-shirt, and also a flattering one, overestimated how many observers noticed it. People watching a recording of someone *else* wearing the shirt estimated about right. The often-quoted "about 50% predicted vs. about 25% actual" figures were *not* verified in what I retrieved; a secondary source says roughly "twice as many." — [Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky 2000, JPSP 78(2):211-222, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10707330/); [Psychology Today (secondary)](https://psychologytoday.com/us/blog/mental-mishaps/202111/why-some-people-assume-everyone-is-looking-them)
- **Faces are poor emotion diagnostics (2019 review):** Facial movements are not reliable or specific enough across contexts, people and cultures to diagnose emotional states. A scowl often communicates something other than anger, and a smile may signal submission instead of happiness. Studies in remote cultures found no facial configuration specific to a given emotion, though people could still infer some social meanings. Coverage at the time warned that emotion-detection apps were "built on outdated science." — [Barrett et al. 2019, Psychological Science in the Public Interest (APS)](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/emotional-expressions-reconsidered-challenges-to-inferring-emotion-from-human-facial-movements.html); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6640856); [Waisman Center news](https://www.waisman.wisc.edu/2019/07/24/emotion-detection-applications-built-on-outdated-science-report-warns/)
- **Gender and anger (older, 2008):** In three studies, both male and female evaluators gave lower status to angry female professionals than to angry male professionals. Women's anger was attributed to internal characteristics ("she's an angry person") and men's to circumstances, an attribution-error pattern. When the woman gave an external reason for her anger, the bias disappeared. — [Brescoll & Uhlmann 2008, Psychological Science 19(3):268-275, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18315800/)
- **Contested replication (2021):** A conceptual replication (N = 1,095) found that both Black and White women shown as angry at work were rated *more* favorably than White men. Evaluations were moderated by news engagement (the #MeToo period) and by beliefs that workplace opportunity is gendered. This suggests the effect depends on social context. — [McCormick-Huhn & Shields 2021, Frontiers in Psychology (PDF)](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.594260/pdf); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7947812)
- **Race and anger perception (older, 2003-2008):** Higher *implicit* prejudice was associated with readier perception of anger in Black faces, but not in comparable White faces. Observers high in implicit prejudice also categorized racially ambiguous angry faces as Black more often. — [Hugenberg & Bodenhausen 2003, Psychological Science (Kellogg record)](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2003/facing-prejudice-implicit-prejudice-and-the-perception-of-facial/?p=1); [Hutchings & Haddock 2008, JESP (SSOAR)](https://www.ssoar.info/ssoar/handle/document/26287)
- **Counterpoint on facial structure (2022):** Neutral European American faces structurally resemble anger more, and fear less, than African American faces. The authors argue the "angry Black" association is therefore socially constructed, not driven by facial structure. — [Adams et al. 2022, Affective Science](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42761-021-00091-5)
- **The "Angry Black Woman" stereotype (2022):** Across two studies (n = 555), observers made more internal attributions for anger expressed by Black women, which led to worse performance evaluations and lower ratings of leadership capability. Study 2 supported stereotype activation as the mechanism. — [Motro, Evans, Ellis & Benson 2022, J. Applied Psychology 107(1):142-152 (summary)](https://www.ethicalpsychology.com/2022/04/race-and-reactions-to-womens.html); [Newswise release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/it-costs-to-be-an-angry-black-woman-for-black-women-in-business)
- **Neurodivergence (2017):** Non-autistic observers were less willing to interact with autistic children and adults after thin-slice exposure to real-world video. These impressions formed quickly and did not improve with more exposure. The bias *disappeared* when judgments were based on conversational content without audio-visual cues, so it was driven by delivery style, not substance. — [Sasson et al. 2017, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep40700); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5286449)
- **Double empathy problem:** Under Milton's account, misunderstanding between autistic and non-autistic people runs both ways. It reflects mismatched expectations and styles, not a deficit on one side. — [Frontiers for Young Minds 2021, "Double Empathy"](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frym.2021.554875/pdf); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/autism-human-connection-and-the-double-empathy-problem)
- **Culture moderates negotiation effects:** In the 2020 meta-analysis, culture was among the moderators of how negative emotional displays affect concessions and trust. — [Sharma et al. 2020](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-effects-of-emotional-expressions-in-negotiation-a-meta-analys/)
- **Delay is misattributed to the person:** Listeners blame a partner's personality for transmission delay (see Q5), another attribution error. — [Schoenenberg et al. 2014, IJHCS](https://researchr.org/publication/SchoenenbergRK14)

### Inferences
- **Steady could fuel users' own illusion of transparency and spotlight effect.** Users already overestimate how visible their nervousness or irritation is. Frequent real-time warnings could confirm that belief and raise self-focus, much like the mirror-anxiety mechanism in Q5. The Savitsky & Gilovich intervention, if it holds up, suggests that telling users "this is probably less visible than you think" can itself help.
- **A face-based classifier carries the same reading biases human observers have.** Barrett et al. 2019 implies that "your face looks angry" is a probabilistic guess about how *some* observers may read a face, not a fact about the user's emotion. Model outputs should be framed as "may be read as...". Labels drawn from human annotators could encode the race-linked anger bias (Hugenberg & Bodenhausen) or structural confounds (Adams et al. 2022).
- **The same nudge has different stakes for different users.** For women and Black women, observed anger costs more (Brescoll & Uhlmann; Motro et al.), so a "you seem angry" nudge carries higher stakes, and it also risks pushing these users to self-police harder. Giving a brief external reason for frustration reduced the gender penalty in Brescoll & Uhlmann, which suggests a "name the cause" type of nudge.
- **Autistic users risk being flagged for style rather than content.** Sasson et al. found that style, not content, drives the negative impressions, so a coach that flags atypical prosody or gaze could reinforce a one-sided norm (the double empathy concern). Consider opt-in modes or settings designed for neurodivergent users.

### Gaps
- **Resting or neutral faces read as negative:** I did not retrieve sourced data on how often neutral faces are misread as angry or contemptuous (for example, Said, Sebe & Todorov 2009 on neutral faces' structural resemblance to emotions, or "resting face" studies). Only the Adams et al. 2022 structural finding was sourced.
- **Classic fundamental attribution error studies** (Jones & Harris 1967; Ross 1977) were not searched. The attribution-error evidence above comes from Brescoll & Uhlmann, Motro et al., and Schoenenberg.
- **Jack et al. 2012 (PNAS) on cultural specificity of facial expressions** was not verified, nor was the oft-quoted figure that scowls accompany anger only about 30% of the time.
- **East Asian vs. Western display rules and high- vs. low-context norms** in meetings were not retrieved.
- **The original 1998 paper's** numbers came through Wikipedia only. Direct replications of the spotlight effect were not checked.

## 5. Video meetings: camera gaze, latency and turn-taking, self-view and mirror anxiety, reduced nonverbal bandwidth, Zoom fatigue, and reading expressions on small tiles

### Takeaway
Bailenson's 2021 "nonverbal overload" paper is a *theory* paper. It names four causes of Zoom fatigue: close-up gaze, effortful nonverbal signaling, the "all-day mirror," and reduced mobility. A large survey (N = 9,787) later supported it and found women more fatigued, with mirror anxiety as a key mediator. Cameras raise fatigue, especially for women and newcomers, and the fatigue lowers voice and engagement. Even 30-70 ms of latency disrupts turn-taking rhythm. Longer delays (about 1.2 s) lead listeners to judge the *person* as less attentive and less conscientious. Perceived eye contact is best when the speaker looks slightly *below* the camera. Some 2025 evidence finds mirror anxiety unrelated to fatigue, so the self-view mechanism is not settled.

### Cited Findings
- **Theory (2021):** Bailenson's paper proposes four causes of Zoom fatigue: (1) excessive close-up eye gaze, with faces at a size that mimics intimate personal space; (2) cognitive load from producing and interpreting nonverbal cues; (3) the "all-day mirror" of self-view, since seeing oneself increases self-criticism; and (4) reduced mobility. Stanford explicitly notes that these arguments had "yet to be directly tested." — [Bailenson 2021, Technology, Mind, and Behavior, DOI 10.1037/tmb0000030 (VHIL)](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/social-interaction/nonverbal-overload-theoretical-argument-causes-zoom-fatigue); [Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2021/02/four-causes-zoom-fatigue-solutions)
- **Large-sample test (2023):** Fauville, Luo, Queiroz, Lee, Bailenson and Hancock surveyed 9,787 participants and found support for nonverbal overload. People who used video-conferencing more often, for longer, and with fewer breaks reported more fatigue. Women reported more fatigue because they experienced these mechanisms more strongly. — [Fauville et al. 2023, Computers in Human Behavior Reports (VHIL)](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/videoconferencing/video-conferencing-usage-dynamics-and-nonverbal-mechanisms); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/64e3fedfe38e481fbbb927ff75deb0cc)
- **Mirror anxiety and gender (2021 preprint, snippet only):** Mirror anxiety, measured by self-report and by linguistic analysis of open-ended responses, mediated the gender gap in Zoom fatigue. — [Stanford SML publication page](https://sml.stanford.edu/publications/2023/video-conferencing-usage-dynamics-and-nonverbal-mechanisms-exacerbate-zoom)
- **Self-view while listening (2024):** Looking at one's self-view more often while *listening* to others indirectly increased negative affect (Studies 1 and 2) and exhaustion (Study 2) by raising public self-awareness. The authors note that earlier studies did not control whether self-view was on or off. — [Business & Information Systems Engineering 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12599-024-00874-7)
- **Contrary evidence (2025):** A study comparing VR and 2D video-conferencing found that only feeling physically trapped predicted video-conferencing fatigue. Mirror anxiety, producing nonverbal cues, and hyper-gaze were *not* related. — [Cyberpsychology: Journal of Psychosocial Research on Cyberspace](https://cyberpsychology.eu/article/view/40292)
- **"Hide self-view" is advice, not a tested intervention:** Bailenson recommends hiding self-view once framing is set. — [Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2021/02/four-causes-zoom-fatigue-solutions)
- **Camera-on field experiment (2021):** A 4-week within-person experiment (103 employees, 1,408 daily observations, at one health-care services company) manipulated camera use. Camera-on days increased fatigue, which in turn reduced voice and engagement in meetings; there was no direct effect. Cameras were more fatiguing for women and newer employees, which the authors attribute to higher self-presentation costs. — [Shockley et al. 2021, J. Applied Psychology (UArizona record)](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-fatiguing-effects-of-camera-use-in-virtual-meetings-a-within-/); [UGA News](https://news.uga.edu/cameras-not-meetings-cause-zoom-fatigue/); [SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/study-camera-use-virtual-meetings-leads-to-fatigue-disengagement)
- **Small latency disrupts turn-taking (2021):** Transmission lags of about 30-70 ms disrupt the brain's rhythm-tracking of conversation. Turn-initiation times were longer over Zoom, which forces people onto less automatic cognitive mechanisms. Evidence here is from press coverage only. — [U. Michigan News, "Zoom disrupts the rhythm of conversation"](https://news.umich.edu/zoom-disrupts-the-rhythm-of-conversation); [Medical Xpress reprint, Boland et al., J. Exp. Psych: General, DOI 10.1037/xge0001150](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2021-11-disrupts-rhythm-conversation.pdf)
- **Latency causes conversational trouble (2021):** A conversation-analysis study of 25 UK video clinical consultations found that latency produces silences where talk should occur, overlapping talk, and difficulty returning to one speaker at a time. — [Seuren et al. 2021, Journal of Pragmatics (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7819463)
- **Delay is blamed on the person (older, 2014):** With 1.2 s transmission delays, partners were rated less attentive, less extraverted and less conscientious; press coverage adds "less friendly." Perceived call quality was *not* affected for delays up to 1,200 ms, so listeners blamed the person, not the connection. The study may have been audio or teleconference-based rather than video. — [Schoenenberg, Raake & Koeppe 2014, Int. J. Human-Computer Studies 72(5):477-487](https://researchr.org/publication/SchoenenbergRK14); [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/awkward-pauses-in-online-calls-make-us-see-people-differently-26073)
- **Camera gaze (2024):** Perceived eye contact was strongest when the speaker looked about 2 degrees of visual angle *below* the camera lens, not straight into it. — [arXiv 2404.17104, "Don't Look at the Camera: Achieving Perceived Eye Contact"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.17104)
- **Gaze in tiled layouts (2022):** Viewers judged gaze as direct when it was aimed slightly upward, within about ±4 degrees. The optimal gaze direction was somewhat higher for tiles at the bottom of the screen. Whether a viewer feels looked at depends on their tile position and camera placement. — [Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications 2022, "Perception of direct gaze in a video-conference setting: the effects of position and size"](https://cognitiveresearchjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41235-022-00418-1)

### Inferences
- **Latency produces false negatives about the user.** Pauses, slow replies and talk-overs on video may come from the network, yet listeners attribute them to the user's attentiveness or personality. A coach should not flag "slow responses" or "interrupting" without accounting for latency. It could also usefully tell users that others may misread their lag.
- **Self-view and the coach compete for the same attention.** A coach that sends users back to their own face may recreate the self-view or public self-awareness mechanism linked to negative affect and exhaustion, especially for women and newcomers. Fewer, higher-confidence nudges, and nudges that don't require looking at oneself, are consistent with this evidence.
- **Gaze advice should be calibrated.** A "look at the camera" nudge may be slightly wrong: looking just below the lens reads better. Perceived eye contact also depends on the viewer's tile, which the sender cannot control.
- **The mechanisms are contested.** The 2025 null on mirror anxiety and Bailenson's own caveat that the theory was untested mean the team should treat nonverbal overload as plausible, not settled.

### Gaps
- **How expressions are read on small tiles:** I found no study measuring emotion-recognition accuracy by tile size or resolution.
- **Tomprou et al. 2021** (video vs. audio-only collective intelligence) was not retrieved, and neither was other work on whether video improves or harms group performance.
- **Beyea et al. 2025 meta-analysis** of Zoom fatigue antecedents is referenced in the 2025 study, but its findings were not retrieved.
- **2025-2026 studies** on AI meeting assistants, real-time coaching or on-device feedback effects in meetings were not found.
- **Boland et al.:** Full text not retrieved; it was dated 2021 online, and the "2022" in the brief may reflect the print date.

## 6. Repair: which behaviors de-escalate or repair a moment that landed badly, and how quickly repair needs to happen

### Takeaway
Gottman's research holds that stable couples make frequent repair attempts during conflict, such as owning part of the problem, and that failed repair predicts poor outcomes. Most accessible material on this is institute-authored. On apologies, timing evidence conflicts. One study found that *later* apologies, given after the offended party has had a chance to voice their view, raised satisfaction more. Another found earlier apologies better for willingness to reconcile. For trust repair, apology works better for competence lapses than for integrity violations, and silence or reticence is a poor response. I found no evidence specifying a seconds- or minutes-level repair window in meetings.

### Cited Findings
- **Repair attempts:** Couples who handle conflict well make frequent "repair attempts," efforts to de-escalate tension mid-discussion, such as "Hey, this isn't all your fault, I know that part of this is me." Unhealthy couples don't repair, or their repairs fail. Sources are popular or institute-authored. — [Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/us/blog/intersections/202009/four-ways-ruin-and-repair-your-relationship); [Gottman Institute research](https://www.gottman.com/research/); [Gottman blog](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-research-predicting-divorce-among-newlyweds-from-the-first-three-minutes-of-a-marital-conflict-discussion/)
- **Openings matter (older, 1999):** The first 3 minutes of a conflict discussion predicted 6-year outcomes. The 1999 abstract itself does not discuss repair. — [Carrère & Gottman 1999, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10526767/)
- **Apology timing (older, 2005):** Later apologies, as opposed to earlier ones, raised satisfaction with how a conflict was resolved more. This conflicts with Tomlinson et al. (2004), who found earlier apologies more effective for willingness to make amends. — [Frantz & Bennigson 2005, "Better late than early," JESP 41:201-207](https://opact.sdu.edu.tr/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c454t-8883848902e9b013edc4d8b41ab72d513bdb036d1b4781f4ab51eb6ae6c80a703/Description); [later paper summarizing the conflict, J. Business & Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-015-9431-8)
- **Apology vs. denial (older, 2004):** Trust was repaired more successfully when the mistrusted party *apologized* for competence-related violations. The integrity-violation result was cut off in the snippet. — [Kim, Ferrin, Cooper & Dirks 2004, J. Applied Psychology 89:104-118 (PDF)](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/context/lkcsb_research/article/3367/viewcontent/RemovingShadowSuspicion_afv.pdf)
- **Silence is a poor repair (older, 2007):** Reticence, meaning neither apologizing nor denying, is a suboptimal response to an integrity violation. — [Ferrin et al. 2007, "Silence Speaks Volumes" (PDF)](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/context/lkcsb_research/article/3525/viewcontent/SilenceSpeaksVolumes_2007.pdf)
- **Explaining the cause:** When an angry woman gave an *external* explanation for her anger, the gender status penalty disappeared. — [Brescoll & Uhlmann 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18315800/)
- **Redirecting emotion from person to issue:** Emotion aimed at the offer or issue, not the person, is read as more informative and less of a relational attack. — [EASI chapter](https://dare.uva.nl/document/2/61635)
- **Trust buffers sarcasm:** When sarcasm passes between people who trust each other, it raises creativity without raising conflict, so prior relationship capital acts as a buffer. — [Huang, Gino & Galinsky 2015](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/55dcde36e4b0df55a96ab220/t/569e634a05f8e2b24db420a4/1453220682892/Huang+Gino+Galinsky+OBHDP+2015.pdf)
- **Positive contagion as a lever:** Positive mood contagion improved cooperation and reduced conflict in work groups. — [Barsade 2002](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/2002/barsade2002a.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Candidate repair nudges grounded in the evidence above:**
  - Own part of the problem (Gottman-style repair).
  - Give a brief external reason for visible frustration (Brescoll & Uhlmann).
  - Redirect from person to issue (EASI).
  - Apologize for competence lapses rather than staying silent (Kim et al.; Ferrin et al.).
  - Let the other person voice their view before apologizing, since early apologies may seem premature (Frantz & Bennigson).
- **Timing pulls two ways.** Negativity bias and the first-3-minutes finding argue for repairing early, before negative impressions harden. Frantz & Bennigson suggest an apology lands better after the other person feels heard. A reasonable reading is: acknowledge or ask a question quickly, then apologize once the concern is voiced. This is my synthesis, not a tested protocol.
- **"Lower your voice" and "pause" are untested in this material.** They fit contagion (calm displays spread) but have no direct support here.

### Gaps
- I found no study specifying *how quickly* repair must happen (for example, within seconds or within the same meeting) to be effective.
- I found no primary peer-reviewed evidence on the effectiveness of specific de-escalation behaviors (pausing, lowering vocal volume, asking a question, acknowledging) in workplace or video settings. The Gottman repair research was available only in popular or institute summaries.
- Apology research beyond the items above (meta-analyses of apology components; apology in video vs. face-to-face) was not retrieved.
- I found no research on whether *real-time machine prompts* to repair improve outcomes, or whether they come across as inauthentic to counterparts.
