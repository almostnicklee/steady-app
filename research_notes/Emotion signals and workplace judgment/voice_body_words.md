## 1. Vocal acoustics of emotion: pitch, loudness, rate, voice quality and spectral balance for anger (hot vs cold), anxiety, sadness, contempt and calm; individual differences and baselines

### Takeaway
High-arousal states (hot anger, fear or panic, joy) reliably raise pitch level and variability, loudness, speech rate and high-frequency energy (a flatter spectral tilt, which sounds "sharper"). Sadness lowers all of these. But cold anger, contempt and sarcasm do not follow the "louder, higher, faster" pattern: they are often lower-pitched, slower or flat. This means Steady's current acoustic features mostly track arousal, not hostility. Speaker-specific traits, especially for valence (how positive or negative someone sounds), are large enough that per-person baselines are clearly needed.

### Cited Findings
**Foundational reviews (older, still the main references)**
- Juslin & Laukka (2003), "Communication of Emotions in Vocal Expression and Music Performance: Different Channels, Same Code?", *Psychological Bulletin* 129(5):770–814 (DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.129.5.770). It meta-analyses decoding accuracy for anger, fear, happiness, love-tenderness and sadness within and across cultures, and concludes that these emotions are conveyed by patterns of cues in pitch, intensity, voice quality and duration. — [search summary of the paper and citing works](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/cross-cultural-emotion-recognition-and-in-group-advantage-in-voca/); [Pell et al. 2009, citing J&L](https://www.mcgill.ca/pell_lab/files/pell_lab/pell_paulmann_dara_alasseri__kotz_2009_0.pdf)
- Direction of cues, as later papers summarise the J&L tradition. Anger, fear and happiness go with a moderate-to-high mean F0 (pitch) and a fast speech rate. Sadness goes with a low mean F0 and a slow rate. Mean pitch, pitch range and speech rate "appear to differentiate well". — [Pell et al. 2009](https://www.mcgill.ca/pell_lab/files/pell_lab/pell_paulmann_dara_alasseri__kotz_2009_0.pdf); [Laukka et al. 2011](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~julia/papers/laukkaetal11.pdf)
- Anger and happiness usually show high F0 variation, while fear and sadness often show less. Anger is linked with high vocal intensity. Sadness is linked with a slow rate and low intensity. The literature notes exceptions to these patterns. — [Morningstar & Dirks 2017 (review section)](https://www.psych.mcgill.ca/labs/dirks/docs/MorningstarDirks2017.pdf); [PMC3689256](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3689256)
- Scherer's 1986 *Psychological Bulletin* review pointed out a gap: listeners recognise vocal emotion well, yet the evidence that the acoustics actually differ between emotions was weak. Scherer (2003, *Speech Communication* 40:227–256) proposes Brunswik's lens model as the framework, separating the speaker's acoustic cues from the listener's perception of them. — [UNIGE archive record](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:102083)

**Hot vs cold anger (Banse & Scherer 1996, *JPSP* 70(3):614–636; older)**
- The study ran digital acoustic analysis on 224 actor portrayals of 14 emotions. Emotions were chosen in "families" (for example hot vs cold anger, panic fear vs anxiety, elation vs happiness) so that intensity cues could be separated from quality cues. A discriminant analysis classified the portrayals with "remarkably high hit rates", and its confusions closely mirrored those of human listeners. — [Banse & Scherer 1996 (abstract via search)](https://psychologie.uni-bonn.de/en/department/departments/social-and-legal-psychology/uploads-fuer-die-allgemeine-i/1996bansescherer_acoustic-profiles-in-vocal-emotion-expression.pdf/@@download/file/1996BanseScherer_Acoustic%20Profiles%20in%20Vocal%20Emotion%20Expression.pdf)
- Hot anger has a higher F0, more overall energy and more energy concentrated above 1000 Hz (that is, a flatter spectral tilt). — [secondary review summarising Banse & Scherer, via search](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3192404)
- Hot anger shows higher mean F0, F0 variability and F0 range. Cold anger shows *lower* mean F0 with no change in variability or range. "Hot and cold anger are externalised by very different acoustic cues." — [ICPhS 2019 paper](https://assta.org/proceedings/ICPhS2019Microsite/pdf/full-paper_599.pdf)

**Anxiety (evidence conflicts)**
- Laukka et al. (2008), "In a Nervous Voice", *J. Nonverbal Behavior*, analysed pitch, loudness, voice quality and timing in the speech of people with social phobia. Secondary sources disagree about its results: one reports *decreased* mean F0 and fewer silent pauses, while another review says most anxiety studies report *increased* F0 and that pause time and pause count rise with anxiety. Treat the direction of the F0 and pause effects for anxiety as contested. — [Laukka et al. 2008 (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10919-008-0055-9); [Vocal Signature of Social Anxiety, arXiv 2207.08534](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.08534); [Archives of Acoustics 2018 review](https://www.czasopisma.pan.pl//Content/109196/PDF/aoa.2018.125156.pdf)
- In one study, people whose social anxiety dropped after treatment showed less relative silence afterwards. — [arXiv 2207.08534](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.08534)

**Contempt and sarcasm (verbal hostility with "cool" prosody)**
- Rockwell (2000) found that a slower tempo, greater intensity and a *lower* pitch level were significant markers of sarcasm. — [Rockwell 2000, J. Psycholinguistic Research](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1005120109296)
- In Canadian English, utterances heard as sarcastic had a lower mean pitch, less pitch variation and a slower rate. — [Cheang & Pell 2008](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~julia/papers/cheangDell08.pdf)
- Across languages, a slower rate is the most consistent sarcasm cue. F0 mean and range depend on the language: Italian sarcasm is reportedly *higher* pitched. In Cantonese (Lan & Mok 2025), sarcasm is slower, lower in mean F0, narrower in F0 range and lower in amplitude, and listeners could tell it from sincerity on acoustics alone. — [Lan & Mok 2025](https://ling.cuhk.edu.hk/people/peggy/LanMok_L&S_Sarcasm_2025.pdf); [arXiv 2408.14892](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.14892)
- A 2026 perception study using synthetic speech found that higher loudness, especially combined with a flat pitch contour, was the most reliable cue to perceived sarcasm. This partly conflicts with the older "lower pitch" findings. — [arXiv 2606.09717](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.09717)
- No search result isolated vocal cues specific to contempt (as opposed to sarcasm). See Gaps.

**GeMAPS / eGeMAPS**
- Eyben et al. (2016), *IEEE Trans. Affective Computing* 7(2):190–202 (DOI 10.1109/TAFFC.2015.2457417). This is a deliberately small acoustic parameter set. Parameters were chosen for (a) their potential to index the physiological changes emotion causes in voice production, (b) proven value in earlier studies and automatic extractability, and (c) theoretical significance. It is meant as a common baseline so that differences between studies do not come from different feature sets or implementations. The reference implementation ships with openSMILE, and the paper reports performance close to the much larger INTERSPEECH challenge feature sets. — [Eyben et al. 2016 (PDF host)](https://msp.utdallas.edu/publications/Eyben_2016.pdf); [U. Twente record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/the-geneva-minimalistic-acoustic-parameter-set-gemaps-for-voice-r/)

**Individual differences and the case for baselines**
- In speech emotion recognition, moving from speaker-independent to speaker-dependent models gives relative gains of up to 30% in concordance correlation for *valence*, but under 4% for arousal and dominance. The authors attribute this to valence cues carrying more speaker-specific traits. — [Sridhar & Busso, arXiv 2201.07876](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2201.07876)
- Speaker-dependent recognition usually beats speaker-independent recognition, and the biggest gains go to speakers whose baseline recognition rate was low. — [Rybka & Janicki 2013](https://yadda.icm.edu.pl/baztech/element/bwmeta1.element.baztech-f3db2a83-fafb-43d4-9d45-88436b93ca9f/c/Rybka.pdf)
- Personalisation methods help: pre-training a speech encoder with speaker embeddings shrinks the personalisation gap as the number of training speakers grows. Separately, speaker-normalisation methods improve both speaker-independent and speaker-dependent results. — [Tran, Yin & Soleymani, arXiv 2309.02418](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/2309.02418); [Gat et al., arXiv 2202.01252](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.01252)
- Wagner et al. (2023, IEEE TPAMI) found transformer speech-emotion models fair across sex/gender *groups* but **not across individual speakers**. — [arXiv 2203.07378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07378)

### Inferences
- Steady's loudness, pitch (semitones above baseline), sharpness (spectral tilt) and rate features are essentially an **arousal detector**. They should flag hot anger, panic and excitement alike, and cannot tell anger from enthusiasm without lexical or contextual input. This is consistent with the Sridhar & Busso finding that arousal transfers across speakers better than valence.
- **Cold anger, contempt and sarcasm will mostly slip past the acoustic thresholds.** They tend toward lower or flat pitch and a slower rate, while loudness findings are mixed. The phrase matcher is therefore the main detector for these stances. A "slow and flat, but hostile words" pattern may be worth flagging as its own signature.
- Measuring pitch in semitones relative to the user's baseline is the right choice. Semitones are a ratio scale, so they partly remove the roughly one-octave difference in typical F0 between adult male and female voices (standard phonetics, not sourced this session). The strong individual effects suggest baselines for loudness, rate and spectral tilt as well, ideally per device and per room.
- The anxiety evidence is too mixed to support a confident "anxious voice" flag from F0 or pauses alone.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm exact values from the primary papers. These include Juslin & Laukka's cue table (for example whether anger shows "high" high-frequency energy, or fear "low" F0 variability) and their overall decoding-accuracy index, Banse & Scherer's per-emotion listener accuracy (the commonly cited figure of about 48% against about 7% chance for 14 categories comes from memory and is **unverified**), and their acoustic values for contempt, anxiety and boredom.
- GeMAPS contents were not confirmed this session. From background knowledge (unverified): about 62 parameters in GeMAPS and 88 in eGeMAPS, including F0 in semitones from 27.5 Hz, loudness, jitter, shimmer, HNR, H1–H2, H1–A3, alpha ratio, Hammarberg index, spectral slopes over 0–500 Hz and 500–1500 Hz, formants, MFCC1–4 (eGeMAPS only), rate of loudness peaks and voiced-segment length. Check the [Eyben 2016 PDF](https://msp.utdallas.edu/publications/Eyben_2016.pdf) before relying on these.
- No source was found on the acoustic profile of "calm" or "warmth" (affiliative tone) in workplace speech.
- No source was found that quantifies how much loudness, F0 or rate vary within one person across days or meetings (test-retest variability). Such data would be needed to set thresholds for "meaningfully above baseline".

## 2. How accurate are humans and machines at recognising emotion from voice alone? Known confounds (microphone distance, room, language, gender, accent)

### Takeaway
Humans recognise vocal emotion well above chance but imperfectly, and they do better within their own culture. Machines do well on arousal but worse on valence, and their accuracy drops sharply across datasets, recording conditions and speakers. Background noise alone (the Lombard effect) produces the same acoustic changes Steady treats as signs of escalation.

### Cited Findings
**Humans**
- Laukka & Elfenbein (2021), *Emotion Review* 13(1):3–11. A meta-analysis of 37 cross-cultural studies (speech prosody and non-linguistic vocalisations) covering expressers from 26 cultural groups. Recognition was more accurate when expresser and listener shared a culture (an in-group advantage). Cultural distance (Hofstede dimensions) correlated negatively with accuracy and positively with the size of the in-group advantage. — [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/cross-cultural-emotion-recognition-and-in-group-advantage-in-voca/); [SAGE figshare](https://sage.figshare.com/collections/Cross-Cultural_Emotion_Recognition_and_In-Group_Advantage_in_Vocal_Expression_A_Meta-Analysis/4858011)
- A later study says that meta-analysis supported cross-cultural recognition of *negative* emotions, with positive emotions more variable (snippet truncated). — [PMC9550067](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9550067/)
- Scherer, Banse & Wallbott (2001) found that accuracy fell as the listener's language grew more different from German, even though the stimuli were language-free speech. A secondary source reports 66% accuracy across countries, but which study that figure comes from was not confirmed. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00353/full)
- Positive non-linguistic vocalisations were recognised above chance mainly within the same cultural group. — [Laukka et al. 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3728469/)

**Machines**
- Wagner et al. (2023), "Dawn of the Transformer Era in Speech Emotion Recognition: Closing the Valence Gap", IEEE TPAMI 45(9). wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT models fine-tuned on MSP-Podcast reached a valence concordance correlation of about .638, the best reported without explicit text input. Cross-corpus tests used IEMOCAP and MOSI. The models were more robust than a CNN baseline and fair across sex groups, but not across individual speakers. — [arXiv 2203.07378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07378)
- Most speech-emotion work trains and tests on a single corpus and language. Cross-corpus transfer is poor because corpora differ in language, culture, recording conditions and size. — [Zhang et al. 2021, Frontiers in Neurorobotics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8666588)
- Performance on one dataset does not reliably carry over to others, including for wav2vec, WavLM, VGGish and CLAP embeddings (Microsoft, JASA abstract 2024). — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=1155873)
- Gender, phoneme, subject and recording device are named as nuisance factors that hurt cross-corpus performance, especially with small datasets. — [ADDoG, arXiv 1903.12094](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.12094)

**Acoustic confounds that look like emotion**
- Lombard effect: in background noise, speakers involuntarily raise intensity and F0, shift energy from low to mid and high frequencies (a flatter spectral tilt) and lengthen vowels. — [Wikipedia: Lombard effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lombard_effect); [PMC5398925](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5398925/)
- A 2008 study found that noise raised energy and mean F0 and *reduced* spectral tilt. The task affected energy and tilt more strongly than F0. — [Lu & Cooke, competing-talker study](https://personal.utdallas.edu/~hynek/citing_papers/Lu_Speaking_in_the_presence_of_a_competing_talker.pdf)
- People respond to noise by different amounts. — [PMC9913286](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9913286)

### Inferences
- **The Lombard effect is the single most important false-positive risk for Steady.** A noisy café, a laggy call where the user cannot hear, or a hard-of-hearing participant will push loudness, pitch and "sharpness" up together, which is exactly the "heated" signature. Steady should estimate background noise and the far-end level and discount readings accordingly.
- Microphone distance and gain change measured loudness directly. In free-field conditions, sound level drops by about 6 dB per doubling of distance (standard acoustics, not sourced this session). Directional microphones up close also boost low frequencies (the proximity effect), which shifts spectral tilt. A user leaning toward the laptop could therefore read as "louder and less sharp". Loudness and tilt should be normalised per session or per device.
- Because cross-corpus and cross-speaker accuracy is poor, on-device thresholds learned on acted corpora will generalise badly. Personal baselines plus conservative, user-facing wording ("your voice is louder than your usual") are better supported than emotion labels ("you sound angry").
- In-group advantage and language effects suggest that meeting partners from different cultures may read the same prosody differently. Steady's nudges are about how the user sounds to themselves, not about how a specific listener will perceive them.

### Gaps
- No single review was found that measures microphone, room, gender and accent confounds side by side. The confounds above come from separate sources.
- No recent figure was confirmed for human percent-correct from voice alone (the Juslin & Laukka 2003 decoding index could not be verified).
- No study was found on speech emotion recognition accuracy specifically in video meetings (codec compression, echo cancellation, automatic gain control). These processing steps likely change loudness and spectral tilt, but this is unverified.

## 3. Body: posture, gestures, self-touch, crossed arms, leaning, fidgeting, head movements, gaze. Supported vs myth; what a front-facing camera can see

### Takeaway
Single body "tells" such as crossed arms meaning defensive, or gaze aversion and fidgeting meaning lying, are weakly supported or unsupported. The best-supported body cue relevant here is that self-touch rises with *momentary* (state) anxiety, though even this is correlational. Power posing's hormonal and behavioural effects failed to replicate. Facial movements are not reliable read-outs of emotion. A webcam sees mostly the face, head, shoulders and hands raised near the face, and video calls themselves distort gaze and proximity cues.

### Cited Findings
**Crossed arms and deception "tells"**
- Scholars who link arm-crossing to defensiveness offer only sparse evidence. One paper tested arm crossing in three studies (N = 242) and links it to perseverance and persistence, not only defensiveness. The snippet did not report that paper's results. — [core.ac.uk paper on arm crossing](https://core.ac.uk/download/74373521.pdf); [mindbodygreen summary](https://www.mindbodygreen.com/articles/what-do-crossed-arms-mean)
- An analysis of more than 1,300 estimates of 158 commonly cited deception cues (fidgeting, gaze aversion, face touching and so on) found weak or no links to lying. A large meta-analysis found nonverbal deception cues weak and highly variable across situations. — [Earth.com summary of the research](https://www.earth.com/news/scientists-set-the-record-straight-by-clarifying-common-myths-about-body-language); [PMC8229315](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8229315)
- Bond & DePaulo (2006), a meta-analysis of more than 200 studies, put people's lie-detection accuracy at roughly 54%, only slightly above chance. The US GAO reportedly found no reliable evidence that nonverbal behavioural indicators identify deception. (Both are from secondary summaries.) — [Earth.com](https://www.earth.com/news/scientists-set-the-record-straight-by-clarifying-common-myths-about-body-language); [Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/us/blog/spycatcher/202207/debunking-body-language-myths)

**Power posing (a replication failure)**
- Ranehill et al. (2015, *Psychological Science*), a large replication, found no effect of power poses on hormones or on any of three behavioural tasks. It did find an effect on self-reported feelings of power. — [Ranehill et al. 2015](https://www.zne.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:e5fc4c3c-d50e-4aa9-96ab-4389e426d20d/Psychological%20Science-2015-Ranehill-0956797614553946.pdf)
- Simmons & Simonsohn's p-curve found no evidential value once selective reporting was accounted for. Cuddy, Schultz & Fosse (2018) used a p-curve to argue for an effect on *feelings* of power, but critics say that analysis mixed significant effects in opposite directions and included ineligible studies. Repeated power posing also failed to change hormones (PeerJ 2019). — [Simmons & Simonsohn 2017](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Simmons-Simonsohn-2017.pdf); [Data Colada](https://datacolada.org/37); [PeerJ 6726](https://peerj.com/articles/6726); [PMC6585898](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6585898)
- Co-author Dana Carney published a position statement on power poses. The link surfaced in search but its text was not retrieved; it is widely reported as disavowing the effect. — [Carney position PDF](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu:443/dana_carney/pdf_my%20position%20on%20power%20poses.pdf)

**Self-touch and fidgeting (anxiety)**
- In 42 men, conversational self-touch rate correlated with *state* anxiety (r = 0.326) but not trait anxiety. State anxiety was the only significant predictor in a multiple regression. Earlier work found more self-touch in stressful situations, with participants reporting less negative emotional load afterwards. — [Univ. of Aberdeen (AURA)](https://aura.abdn.ac.uk/items/5147ea47-479b-4e75-8326-c672a2dc9109/full)
- A 2022 *J. Nonverbal Behavior* study of face-to-face conversations found that momentary anxiety, not general anxiety, neuroticism or agreeableness, was the main contributor to self-touch. The researchers caution that causation is unknown: self-touch may serve to *regulate* anxiety. — [Springer s10919-022-00402-9](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10919-022-00402-9); [SPSP Character & Context blog](https://spsp.org/news/character-and-context-blog/chu-tong-pang-hands-show-anxiety)

**Faces**
- Barrett et al. (2019), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 20(1):1–68, find that the evidence does not meet the criteria of reliability, specificity, generalisability and validity for reading emotions from facial configurations. By the authors' own summary, people scowl when angry only about 25% of the time on average, and a smile can signal submission rather than happiness. Cowen and colleagues published a commentary disputing parts of this. — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/emotional-expressions-reconsidered-challenges-to-inferring-emotion-from-human-facial-movements.html); [PMC6640856](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6640856); [Waisman Center release](https://www.waisman.wisc.edu/2019/07/24/emotion-detection-applications-built-on-outdated-science-report-warns/)

**Video-meeting constraints**
- Bailenson (2021, *Technology, Mind, and Behavior*) describes "nonverbal overload" in video calls. These include constant close-up eye gaze (in a gallery grid "everyone is looking at everyone, all the time"), face sizes that mimic intimate distance, self-view, and reduced mobility. He recommends hiding self-view, shrinking windows and using an external camera placed farther back. The argument is theoretical rather than experimental. — [Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2021/02/four-causes-zoom-fatigue-solutions); [Bailenson PDF](https://vhil.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj29011/files/media/file/bailenson-apa-nonverbal-overload.pdf?)

### Inferences
- A front-facing laptop or phone camera usually frames the head, face, neck and shoulders. Crossed arms, leaning (except as a change in face size or distance), lower-body fidgeting and most gestures happen out of frame or at the edge. Hands enter the frame mainly during face or hair touching or animated gestures. So the visual signals Steady could realistically track are head pose and nodding, gaze direction, face-to-camera distance and **hand-to-face self-touch**.
- Of these, self-touch is the best-supported anxiety-linked cue, but its correlation is modest (r of about 0.33) and its meaning is ambiguous (self-soothing). If added at all, it should be framed as "you may be tense", never as evidence of defensiveness or dishonesty.
- On video, gaze toward the camera vs the screen is mostly an artefact of camera placement, so "eye contact" metrics will mislead. Similarly, any feature claiming crossed arms or "closed posture = defensive" lacks scientific support and could damage credibility.
- Steady's "power pose before your meeting" style coaching, if considered, should not promise hormonal or performance effects. At most, the evidence supports a contested effect on self-reported feelings of power.

### Gaps
- No direct study was found on what a webcam actually captures (framing statistics) or on camera-height effects on perceived gaze. Points above about framing are inference.
- No meta-analysis was found on head movements (nodding, head tilt) or leaning as markers of warmth or hostility.
- The arm-crossing paper's actual results (three studies, N = 242; possibly Friedman & Elliot 2008 on persistence) were not visible in snippets.

## 4. Words and language: Four Horsemen, "you" vs "I", absolutist words, interruptions, hedges, sarcasm, profanity, LIWC; which phrases are reliably perceived as confrontational, blaming, defensive or dismissive

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence for confrontational language comes from couples-conflict research. Three patterns are linked to observed negativity and to recipients predicting defensiveness: "you"-focused blame ("you-talk"), global or absolutist character attacks ("you always", "you never", "you are…"), and contempt (sarcasm, mockery, superiority). I-language combined with acknowledging the other person's perspective is rated least hostile. Interruptions raise perceived dominance but lower likability, more so for women. Profanity from leaders lowers employee satisfaction on average, though effects vary.

### Cited Findings
**Gottman's Four Horsemen (couples research, 1990s onward)**
- Criticism attacks the person rather than a specific behaviour (a complaint). Contempt takes a position of moral superiority and shows up as sarcasm, mockery, eye-rolling and hostile humour. Defensiveness deflects rather than engaging, and amounts to blaming the partner. Stonewalling means withdrawing, shutting down and not responding, often after feeling overwhelmed. The Gottman Institute calls contempt the single strongest predictor of divorce. — [Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-four-horsemen-contempt-criticism-defensiveness-and-stonewalling/); [Gottman: Four Horsemen](https://www.gottman.com/blog/four-horsemen/)
- Globalising words such as "always", "never" and "you are" turn a complaint into criticism (example: "You're always late"). These examples come from secondary write-ups, not Gottman's own pages. — [Psychology Today](https://psychologytoday.com/sg/blog/couples-thrive/201909/the-4-things-will-hurt-your-relationship-the-most); [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/relationships/4-horseman/)
- In Gottman's observational coding system (SPAFF), negative codes include contempt, belligerence, domineering, anger, fear, defensiveness, whining, sadness and stonewalling. Gottman's group reports more than 90% accuracy in predicting divorce from code ratios. That figure is self-reported, and critics note that SPAFF's psychometric properties have not been fully examined and that samples were self-selected. Johnson's 2002 review even found contempt associated with *lower* ratings on serious consideration of divorce. — [Gottman: empirical basis](https://gottman.com/blog/the-empirical-basis-for-gottman-method-couples-therapy); [Johnson 2002 (Binghamton)](https://www.binghamton.edu/psychology/labs/marriage/documents/johnson_2002.pdf); [Gottman 1999 (Berkeley)](https://bpl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/docs/60-Dysfunctional%20marital%20conflict99.pdf)

**"You" vs "I" language**
- Simmons, Gordon & Chambless (2005, *Psychological Science*) counted pronouns in 59 couples' problem-solving discussions. More I-language and less you-language went with better problem solving and higher marital satisfaction, while you-language was associated with more negativity, criticism and blaming. The study is correlational. — [Simmons et al. 2005 PDF](https://petercgordon.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/17516/2018/08/SimmonsGordonChamblessPsychSci2005.pdf); [PMC4017621](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4017621)
- Biesen et al. (2016) and Williams-Baucom et al. (2010) link you-talk during conflict to worse perceived interaction quality and observed negativity. A 2021 study of 365 couples found that asymmetric partner-focus (you-talk) was dysfunctional in conflict. The proposed mechanism is that you-talk attributes the problem to the partner and implies blame. — [Arizona: "I blame you, I hear you"](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/i-blame-you-i-hear-you-couples-pronoun-use-in-conflict-and-dyadic/); [PMC11845222](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11845222/)
- Rogers, Howieson & Neame (2018, *PeerJ* 6:e4831) had participants rate hypothetical conflict-opening statements. I-language and communicating perspective each reduced perceived hostility, and the benefit of I-language was larger when statements communicated one or more perspectives. The top-rated opener used I-language to state both one's own and the other person's perspective. A you-statement with no perspective was the most likely to provoke a defensive response. The study builds on Kubany's contrast between "I am feeling upset" and "You have made me upset". These are ratings of hypothetical statements, not observed behaviour. — [Rogers et al. 2018 (PMC5961625)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5961625)

**Absolutist words**
- Al-Mosaiwi & Johnstone (2018, *Clinical Psychological Science*) analysed 63 internet forums (more than 6,400 members) with LIWC. Forums about anxiety, depression and suicidal ideation used more absolutist words ("always", "nothing", "completely", "totally", "entire") than control forums. Absolutist words tracked affective-disorder severity better than negative-emotion words did, and appear to reflect thinking style rather than distress. The design is correlational, and forum membership could not be verified. — [APS Observer summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/all-or-nothing-thinking-more-common-in-people-with-anxiety-depression-and-suicidal-ideation.html); [Reading CentAUR](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/73630)

**LIWC**
- Tausczik & Pennebaker (2010, *J. Language & Social Psychology* 29(1):24–54). LIWC counts the percentage of words in psychologically meaningful categories, and its results show attentional focus, emotionality, social relationships, thinking styles and individual differences. For example, in-group words ("we", "us") carry more affiliative meaning than out-group words ("they", "them"). — [Tausczik & Pennebaker 2010 PDF](https://onlinecampus.fernfh.ac.at/pluginfile.php/242855/mod_resource/content/1/Tausczik%20%20Pennebaker%202010_The%20Psychological%20Meaning%20of%20Words.pdf); [CiteDrive record](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/the-psychological-meaning-of-words-liwc-and-computerized-text-analysis-methods/)

**Interruptions and hedges**
- More interruptions produce a curvilinear rise in perceived dominance. Female interrupters in female–female pairs were rated most dominant, and male interrupters in male–female pairs least dominant. — [Youngquist (Oakland Univ.)](https://www.oakland.edu/Assets/upload/docs/COMJRN/youngquist.interruptions.pdf)
- Interrupters gain status and influence but are liked less, with a larger likability penalty for women (from secondary summaries of two experiments; original study not verified). — [secondary summary](https://artfulparent.com/g-two-experiments-found-that-people-who-interrupted-others-during-a-conversation-were-seen-as-having-more-status-and-influence-but-interrupters-especially-women-were-liked-significantly-less-by-observ/); [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/40470974/this-is-why-you-always-get-interrupted-at-work)
- In groups, both men and women interrupt more when the group is mostly male (Small Group Research, 2004). — [Harvard GAP summary](https://gap.hks.harvard.edu/gender-perceived-competence-and-power-displays-examining-verbal-interruptions-group-context)
- What counts as an interruption differs systematically by listener (Hilton, Stanford), and some interruptions are cooperative or rapport-building rather than dominant. — [Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/?p=20602); [UiTM, professional women's interruptions](https://openaccess.library.uitm.edu.my/Record/scopus_10.17507-jltr.1204.09)
- A small-group study found no significant sex differences in the use of interruptions, hedges or tag questions, which supports a "gender similarities" view of powerless speech. — [Communication Research Reports 1997](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01463379709370066)

**Profanity at work**
- Loughborough (July 2026): 5,660 employees in 19 countries were less satisfied with supervisors who swore frequently. People higher in psychopathy or Machiavellianism were more satisfied as profanity rose. In less equal (more hierarchical) countries, a leader's swearing is more likely read as a display of dominance. — [Loughborough press release](https://www.lboro.ac.uk/media-centre/press-releases/2026/july/swearing-boss-might-make-you-work-or-quit/)
- The most common reason for swearing at work is to express negative emotion, especially anger and frustration. Swearing can also bring stress relief, richer communication and social bonding. Expectancy-violation research shows that workplace swearing is not always judged negatively, and some studies link profanity to perceived honesty. — [J. Managerial Psychology](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/JMP-04-2016-0102/full/html); [Communication Studies (expectancy violations)](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/10510974.2011.638411); [Irish Times](https://www.irishtimes.com/business/work/smart-people-understand-why-it-pays-to-swear-at-work-1.3977568)

**Sarcasm** (see Q1 for prosody): sarcasm and mockery are Gottman's defining behaviours of contempt. — [Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-four-horsemen-contempt-criticism-defensiveness-and-stonewalling/)

### Inferences
- Patterns the evidence supports for Steady's phrase list, roughly from strongest to weakest:
  1. **Blaming "you" + global or absolutist framing**: "you always…", "you never…", "you're so…", "you made me…". Supported by Gottman's criticism vs complaint distinction, the you-talk correlations and Rogers et al.
  2. **Contempt markers**: sarcasm, mockery, superiority ("obviously", "as I already explained", "seriously?"), name-calling. These are the strongest predictor of relationship damage in Gottman's (self-reported) work.
  3. **Defensive deflection and counter-blame**: "that's not my fault", "yes, but…", "well, you…", "I was only…". Defensiveness in Gottman's framing equals blaming the partner.
  4. **Dismissal or stonewalling**: "whatever", "fine", "let's just move on", "I'm done", or withdrawal (long silences, a sharp drop in talk-time share).
- Counting "you" alone is noisy, because neutral workplace speech uses "you" for instructions and generic reference. Pair "you" with negative or evaluative predicates or absolutist words. Absolutist words in first-person self-talk ("I always mess up") point to distress rather than hostility, so they call for a different nudge.
- The best-supported *positive* rewrite suggestion is I-language plus acknowledging the other person's perspective ("I understand you see it as X; I see it as Y").
- Interruption counting is defensible as a dominance signal, but gendered perception effects mean feedback should describe what happened ("you started speaking while Sam was mid-sentence 4 times") rather than judge it. Interruptions also need speaker diarisation, which Steady's current features (the user's own talk-time share) only partly provide.
- Keyword matching cannot tell sarcasm apart from its literal meaning. Combining phrase hits with the "slow, flat pitch" prosody signature from Q1 may help, at the cost of false positives.

### Gaps
- The search found no peer-reviewed lexicon or validated list of workplace phrases rated as confrontational, blaming, defensive or dismissive. Almost all phrase-level evidence comes from couples research, which may not transfer fully to colleagues.
- I could not retrieve specific LIWC findings on anger words, second-person pronouns and hostility, or the LIWC-22 category names and base rates.
- Hedges were not confirmed as being perceived as defensive. Only their sex-similarity in use was found.
- Talk-time share: the search found nothing on how unequal talk-time is perceived (for example as dominance or disengagement). Commonly cited work such as Woolley et al. 2010 on equal turn-taking and collective intelligence was not retrieved.
- No held-out validation of Gottman's divorce-prediction accuracy was found, so the reported figure above 90% should be treated as optimistic.

## 5. When face, voice and words conflict, which do listeners weight most? What is wrong with "7-38-55"?

### Takeaway
"7-38-55" (7% words, 38% tone, 55% face) comes from two small 1967 lab studies of single words and photographs judged for like or dislike. Mehrabian himself says it does not apply beyond communicating feelings and attitudes with inconsistent cues. When perceivers judge what someone is actually thinking or feeling in naturalistic speech, verbal content contributes most, then vocal cues, then visual cues.

### Cited Findings
- The ratio combines two 1967 studies (Mehrabian & Wiener; Mehrabian & Ferris). Participants judged a speaker's attitude (like or dislike) from single recorded words spoken in different tones and from photographs. No real conversations took place. — [Wikipedia: Albert Mehrabian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Mehrabian); [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/the-learning-curve/the-7-38-55-rule-debunking-the-golden-ratio-of-conversation)
- Mehrabian has objected to how his work is portrayed: unless a communicator is talking about their feelings or attitudes, the equations do not apply. Lapakko (2007) argues that broad conclusions about communication "simply cannot be derived" from these methodologically limited studies. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Mehrabian); [Lapakko 2007 (core.ac.uk)](https://core.ac.uk/download/214121529.pdf); [Yaffe 2011, ACM Ubiquity](https://ubiquity.acm.org/article.cfm?id=2043156)
- What survives is that tone and nonverbal cues can outweigh words when a feeling-laden message is ambiguous or contradictory. — [U. Minnesota open textbook](https://open.lib.umn.edu/commpractice/chapter/h1-the-power-of-nonverbal-communication-good-and-bad/)
- Hall & Schmid Mast (2007, *Emotion* 7(2):438–446) used an empathic-accuracy design, showing perceivers full video, audio only, transcript only or silent video. Verbal information contributed most to accuracy, vocal nonverbal cues next, and visual cues least (still above zero). When asked to infer *feelings*, perceivers shifted toward visual cues and away from words. — [Serval (UNIL) record](https://serval.unil.ch/notice/serval:BIB_2D283015F24F); [UNIGE archive](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:101109)
- Zaki, Bolger & Ochsner (2009) found auditory, and especially verbal, information critical to empathic accuracy. A 2009 meta-analysis by Hall and colleagues likewise says accuracy in this paradigm depends mainly on verbal cues. — [Zaki et al. 2009](https://scan.psych.columbia.edu/papers/Zaki_et_al_2009.pdf); [Ovid: Unpacking the informational bases of empathic accuracy](https://www.ovid.com/journals/emotn/pdf/10.1037/a0016551~unpacking-the-informational-bases-of-empathic-accuracy)

### Inferences
- For a meeting coach, the evidence supports putting **words first** (the phrase matcher) as the main signal of hostile, blaming or defensive *stance*. Voice is a strong secondary signal of *arousal or intensity*, and camera body cues are weakest.
- When channels conflict (calm words, raised voice; or polite words, sarcastic flat delivery), listeners may lean on the nonverbal channel for *feelings*, per Hall & Schmid Mast and the narrow Mehrabian setting. A "mismatch" flag (benign words with an arousal spike, or hostile words with flat or slow prosody) is plausible but unvalidated.
- Product copy and marketing should avoid citing "7-38-55". It is a recognised myth, and citing it would undercut credibility with informed users.

### Gaps
- No modern meta-analysis was found that measures how much listeners weight each channel when face, voice and words truly *conflict* in workplace or video-call settings.
- The exact contribution percentages in Hall & Schmid Mast (2007) were not visible in snippets.
