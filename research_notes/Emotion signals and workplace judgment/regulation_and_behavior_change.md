## 1. Gross's process model: which strategies work in the moment vs. long term, and what expressive suppression costs

### Takeaway
Across 306 experimental comparisons, cognitive change (reappraisal, especially perspective-taking) is the most reliable family. Distraction helps briefly. Suppressing *expression* does reduce outward display, but the felt emotion stays, and it costs memory, rapport and the partner's physiology, and it predicts burnout at work. How intense the emotion is decides what is feasible: at high intensity people turn to disengagement (distraction), and reappraisal works better at low-to-moderate intensity. A coach that rewards a calmer face or voice could, without meaning to, train expressive suppression.

### Cited Findings
- Webb, Miles & Sheeran (2012, *Psychological Bulletin* 138(4):775–808) pooled 306 experimental comparisons of strategies derived from the process model. By family: attentional deployment d+ = 0.00 overall, response modulation d+ = 0.16, cognitive change small-to-medium. Within attentional deployment, distraction worked (d+ = 0.27) and concentrating on the emotion backfired (d+ = −0.26). Within response modulation, suppressing *expression* worked on the measured outcomes (d+ = 0.32), but suppressing the *experience* or suppressing thoughts did not. Within cognitive change, reappraising the response (d+ = 0.23) was weaker than reappraising the stimulus (d+ = 0.36) or perspective-taking (d+ = 0.45). Moderators included the emotion being regulated and how often the strategy was used. — [Webb, Miles & Sheeran 2012 (ResearchGate PDF)](https://researchgate.net/profile/Paschal_Sheeran/publication/224955638_Dealing_With_Feeling_A_Meta-Analysis_of_the_Effectiveness_of_Strategies_Derived_From_the_Process_Model_of_Emotion_Regulation/links/0912f504f09b780691000000.pdf); [Sussex figshare record](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Dealing_with_feeling_a_meta-analysis_of_the_effectiveness_of_strategies_derived_from_the_process_model_of_emotion_regulation/23396432)
- That meta-analysis was criticized (Augustine & Hemenover 2013), and the authors replied defending its accuracy and generalizability. — [Sussex figshare, reply to Augustine & Hemenover](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Meta-analytic_estimates_predict_the_effectiveness_of_emotion_regulation_strategies_in_the_real_world_reply_to_Augustine_and_Hemenover_2013_/23396420)
- (Older, 2002 review of Richards & Gross 2000 and related work.) Reappraisal lowers both experienced emotion and its expression, with no memory cost. Suppression lowers expression but not experienced emotion, and it impairs memory, mainly for verbal material. — [Gross 2002, Psychophysiology (PDF)](https://www.psychiatry.wisc.edu/courses/Nitschke/seminar/gross_2002_psychophys.pdf); [PubMed 12212647](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12212647/); [APA press release 2000](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2000/09/emotion-memory)
- (Older, 2003.) Gross & John's five studies of individual differences: habitual reappraisal is associated with better well-being and habitual suppression with worse well-being. — [Gross & John 2003 (PDF)](https://onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca/implicitassociationtestsyessir/wp-content/uploads/sites/9812/2025/12/gross-john-2003.pdf)
- (Older, 2003.) Butler et al., "The Social Consequences of Expressive Suppression": people who suppressed reported more distraction during conversation, and secondary summaries report higher blood pressure in suppressors *and in their conversation partners*. The partner blood-pressure detail comes from a secondary source. — [U. Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-social-consequences-of-expressive-suppression/); [ACBS summary](https://contextualscience.org/publications/social_consequences_expressive_suppression)
- Chervonsky & Hunt (2017, *Emotion* 17(4):669–683) meta-analysis: more suppression goes with poorer social outcomes (more negative first impressions, less social support, lower relationship quality). Expressing positive or general emotion goes with better outcomes. Expressing *anger* goes with poorer social well-being. — [PubMed 28080085](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28080085)
- Workplace analogue: Hülsheger & Schewe's (2011) meta-analysis of emotional labor (95 studies, 494 correlations) found that "surface acting", the workplace term for faking or suppressing displayed emotion, correlated ρ ≈ .39–.48 with indicators of impaired well-being and ρ ≈ −.24 to −.40 with job attitudes. Deep acting (changing the feeling itself, which resembles reappraisal) had weak links to impaired well-being and positive links to emotional performance. — [PubMed 21728441](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21728441/); [Maastricht PDF](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/ws/files/74901261/Hulsheger_2011_On_the_costs_and_benefits_of_emotional.pdf); [APS coverage](https://psychologicalscience.org/?p=158053)
- Later work found that surface acting relates to exhaustion indirectly, through psychological effort and lower felt authenticity. — [Maastricht record (Huppertz et al. 2020)](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/why-do-emotional-labor-strategies-differentially-predict-exhausti/)
- Regulation choice depends on intensity (older, 2011). Sheppes et al. found that people prefer engagement reappraisal for low-intensity negative situations and early disengagement through distraction for high-intensity ones, because distraction blocks emotional information before it gathers force. A 2019 extension found that disengagement, whether situation modification or distraction, is preferred for intense stimuli. — [Sheppes et al. 2011 record](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/emotion-regulation-choice/); [PMC3647109](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3647109); [Motivation & Emotion 2019](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11031-019-09811-8)
- A 2025 preregistered direct replication found that "strategy-situation fit" in daily-life emotion regulation (matching the strategy to how controllable the situation is) is associated with well-being. Only the title and abstract-level framing were seen. — [Willroth et al. 2025 (Berkeley PDF)](https://eerlab.berkeley.edu/pdf/papers/2025_Willroth_etal_Preregistered%20Direct%20Replication%20and%20Extension%20of%20The%20Wisdom%20to%20Know%20the%20Difference%20StrategySituation%20Fit%20in%20Emotion%20Regulation%20in%20Daily%20Life%20Is%20Associated%20With%20WellBeing.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Metric-gaming risk (central design issue):** Steady flags drift in face, voice and words. The fastest way for a user to "pass" is to mask expression, which is surface acting. That costs memory (worse recall of the meeting), rapport, the partner's physiology and long-run burnout. Success metrics and nudge copy should reward *recovering* (slower breathing, re-engaging, asking a question) and self-reported change in experience, not only a flat face or voice.
- In the moment, when emotion is high, the realistic options are attentional or physiological disengagement (a breath, looking at notes, a short pause). Reappraisal and perspective-taking are better rehearsed *before* meetings and in post-meeting review, and cued lightly in the meeting only at moderate drift.
- Perspective-taking had the largest reappraisal effect (d+ = 0.45). A prompt like "What might they be worried about?" may be the best fit for a reappraisal cue at moderate intensity.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis was found that tests these strategies in live workplace meetings. The lab effects come mainly from picture or film paradigms.
- The overall cognitive-change d+ from Webb et al. was not in the snippets (described only as small-to-medium).

## 2. Affect labeling: does naming the feeling reduce reactivity, and how should a nudge be phrased?

### Takeaway
Putting feelings into words reduces subjective distress about as much as reappraisal or distraction in lab tasks. It also reduced physiological fear responding a week later in a phobia exposure study. It works *incidentally*: people do not believe it will help, and the effect does not depend on wanting to regulate. The mechanism and best evidence involve the *person* generating the label. Whether an app's label has the same effect has not been tested.

### Cited Findings
- (Older, 2007.) Lieberman et al., "Putting Feelings Into Words": affect labeling is associated with reduced amygdala response, along a proposed pathway from right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (RVLPFC) to medial prefrontal cortex (MPFC) to amygdala. — [Lieberman et al. 2007 (UCLA PDF)](https://teams.semel.ucla.edu/sites/default/files/publications/May%202007%20-%20Putting%20Feelings%20Into%20Words.pdf)
- (Older, 2011.) Across four studies, Lieberman, Inagaki, Tabibnia & Crockett (*Emotion* 11(3):468–480) found that labeling lowered self-reported distress about as much as reappraisal and distraction. Participants did *not* believe labeling would help. Even after it had reduced their distress, they predicted it would increase distress in future. The authors call it an "incidental" regulation process. Labeling also dampened pleasure from positive images, meaning it dampens affect in general rather than only negative affect. — [PubMed 21534661](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21534661/)
- (Older, 2012.) Kircanski, Lieberman & Craske (*Psychological Science*): 88 people with high spider fear were randomized to affect labeling, reappraisal, distraction, or exposure alone. At 1-week follow-up, using a different spider in a different context, the labeling group showed lower skin conductance and marginally more approach than the distraction group. Self-reported fear did *not* differ between groups. More use of anxiety and fear words during exposure predicted larger reductions in fear responding. — [PubMed 22902568](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22902568/); [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt91f398qp/qt91f398qp.pdf?t=nmsjwr)
- Torre & Lieberman (2018, *Emotion Review*) review affect labeling as implicit emotion regulation that can attenuate emotional experience. This is a narrative review, not a meta-analysis. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1754073917742706)
- No peer-reviewed meta-analysis of the behavioral affect-labeling paradigm was found. A self-published, not peer-reviewed repository claims a pooled g = −0.74 for a UCLA subset (k = 5) and should not be relied on. — [awesome.ecosyste.ms listing](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Fmikhaeelatefrizk%2Faffect-labeling-review)
- Related "self-distancing" language (older, 2014): Kross et al. (*JPSP* 106:304–324) found that silently referring to oneself by name or in the non-first person during self-reflection improved regulation in first-impression and public-speaking stressors. Speakers who used their own name were rated better by independent judges and reported less shame and brooding afterwards. Their cardiovascular responses suggested "challenge" rather than "threat". Commentators call for larger preregistered replications. — [Kross et al. 2014 (Berkeley PDF)](https://rascl.berkeley.edu/assets/files/self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_how_you_do_it_matters.pdf); [U. Michigan news](https://prod.lsa.umich.edu/psych/news-events/all-news/archived-news/2014/02/talking-in-the-3rd-person-lowers-anxiety--study.html)

### Inferences
- **Phrasing:** Nudges should invite the user to name the feeling silently ("Name it: ___?") or offer a neutral observation of the signal ("Voice pace up"). They should not state the emotion for the user ("You're angry") or give orders ("Calm down"). Three reasons: (a) the evidence is for self-generated labels, (b) a wrong label from an algorithm may provoke reactance, and (c) evaluative, self-focused feedback lowers feedback effectiveness (see section 7, Kluger & DeNisi).
- Because users expect labeling *not* to work, onboarding should briefly explain the evidence. Otherwise people may skip the step in favor of strategies that feel more effective.
- Post-meeting review can ask for a richer emotion word for flagged moments (frustrated, dismissed, anxious), which builds a personal vocabulary. The dose-response finding in Kircanski et al. (more emotion words, more fear reduction) supports encouraging labeling, though not specifically granularity.
- Self-distancing self-talk ("[Name], what do you need right now?") is silent, so it is feasible in a meeting and could be offered as an option.

### Gaps
- No study was found comparing labels generated by an app or system with labels generated by the person.
- No sourced evidence was retrieved on emotional granularity or differentiation (Barrett, Kashdan) for this note.
- Most labeling studies are single-session lab paradigms, and transfer to live social conflict is untested.

## 3. Evidence-based in-the-moment skills, and which are feasible in a live meeting without others noticing

### Takeaway
The best-supported quick lever is lowering arousal. A 2024 meta-analysis of 154 studies found that arousal-reducing activities (slow breathing, relaxation, mindfulness, time-outs) reduce anger and aggression (g = −0.63), while venting does not. Exhale-focused breathing has RCT and meta-analytic support. DBT's STOP is a ready-made micro-pause, and cognitive defusion is silent and has large lab effects. Several DBT TIPP skills, such as cold water and intense exercise, cannot be done discreetly in a meeting.

### Cited Findings
- Kjærvik & Bushman (2024, *Clinical Psychology Review*) pooled 154 studies (184 samples, N = 10,189). Arousal-decreasing activities reduced anger and aggression, g = −0.63. Arousal-increasing activities were ineffective overall (g = −0.02, 95% CI −0.13 to 0.09). Effects held across gender, age, culture, digital and in-person delivery, group and individual formats, and field and lab settings. — [PubMed 38518585](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38518585/)
- The same team's press release lists effective techniques including deep breathing, relaxation, mindfulness, meditation, slow yoga, progressive muscle relaxation and *taking a time-out*. It also says venting does not reduce anger. — [Ohio State news](https://news.osu.edu/breathe-dont-vent-turning-down-the-heat-is-key-to-managing-anger/); [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/effective-anger-reduction-techniques-revealed-in-comprehensive-review-of-154-studies/)
- Breathwork meta-analysis (Fincham et al. 2023, *Scientific Reports* 13:432; preregistered, PROSPERO CRD42022296709): across 12 RCTs (785 adults), breathwork reduced self-reported stress, g = −0.35 (95% CI −0.55 to −0.14). Effects on anxiety (g = −0.32) and depression (g = −0.40) were similar. Most studies were at moderate risk of bias. — [PMC9828383](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9828383/); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/breathwork-shows-promise-in-reducing-stress-anxiety-and-depression-according-to-new-meta-analysis/)
- Balban et al. (2023, *Cell Reports Medicine*, NCT05304000): a remote RCT of 5 minutes a day for one month. Breathwork, especially exhale-focused "cyclic sighing", improved mood and lowered respiratory rate more than mindfulness meditation (p < .05). Benefits grew with more days of practice. N ≈ 108 according to press coverage. It was a single trial of a daily practice, not an in-the-moment test. — [PubMed 36630953](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/breathwork-may-improve-mood-and-change-physiological-states-more-effectively-than-mindfulness-meditation/)
- DBT STOP (Linehan manual): **S**top, **T**ake a step back, **O**bserve, **P**roceed mindfully. It is designed to keep people from acting on impulsive urges. — [Guilford, Linehan DBT Skills Training Manual excerpt](https://www.guilford.com/add/linehan7_old/lin-p-4teaching.pdf?t=1)
- DBT TIPP: **T**emperature (cold water), **I**ntense exercise, **P**aced breathing, **P**aired muscle relaxation. These distress-tolerance skills are meant to get through a crisis without making it worse, not to solve the problem, and the manual warns against overusing them in place of problem-solving. — [PositivePsychology.com summary of Linehan 2014](https://positivepsychology.com/?p=128627); [Guilford handouts](https://www.guilford.com/add/linehan7_old/lin-p-handouts.pdf?t=1)
- Evidence for DBT skills in general: in BPD, DBT skills use fully mediated decreases in suicide attempts and depression and increases in anger control (older, 2010). A 2014 pilot RCT of DBT skills training in adults with anxiety or depression but not BPD found larger decreases in emotion dysregulation than an activities group (d = 1.86), more skills use (d = 1.02), and skills use mediated the changes. — [Neacsiu, Rizvi & Linehan 2010 (PMC2914145)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2914145); [Neacsiu et al. 2014 (PubMed 24974307)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24974307/)
- Cognitive defusion (ACT): a 2026 meta-analysis (Oppo et al., *Journal of Contextual Behavioral Science*; 12 studies, 22 samples, n = 659) found large reductions in how believable (g = 1.04) and how distressing (g = 1.12) negative self-referential thoughts felt. Word repetition had larger effects than other techniques. These are mostly brief lab inductions with small samples. — [ACBS record](https://contextualscience.org/it/node/51939)
- Anger-management CBT (older meta-analyses): Del Vecchio & O'Leary, CBT d ≈ .68 (18 studies) and cognitive therapy d ≈ .82 (7 studies); DiGiuseppe & Tafrate d ≈ .71 (57 studies); Beck & Fernandez d ≈ .70 (50 studies, 1,640 participants), as summarized in a VA trial protocol. Saini (2009), 96 studies, overall SMD 0.76, with variation by number of sessions, use of manuals and fidelity checks. — [VA trial protocol NCT02157779](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/79/NCT02157779/Prot_SAP_000.pdf); [Saini 2009, JAAPL](https://jaapl.org/content/37/4/473)

### Inferences
- **Feasible discreetly in a meeting:** one or two long exhales, or a physiological sigh done quietly. A STOP micro-pause, e.g. "Let me think about that for a second." A silent label or distanced self-talk. A silent defusion phrase ("I'm noticing the thought that they're dismissing me"). Small opposite-action and muscle moves: lower the voice and slow the pace, unclench the jaw and hands, press the feet into the floor. The last two are sustained by the HRT competing-response logic in section 4.
- **Partly feasible:** a time-out framed as a normal meeting move ("Can I come back to this by email?", a bathroom break, turning the camera off briefly in a video call). The evidence supports time-outs as arousal reduction, but their social cost varies by context.
- **Not feasible live:** cold water and intense exercise from TIPP. Steady can suggest these for *after* a meeting or between back-to-back meetings.
- Venting is not supported, so Steady should not frame post-meeting journaling as "get it out". Structured review (section 9) is better.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests any of these skills *during* live workplace meetings, or tests whether others notice them.
- No controlled evidence was found for the single "physiological sigh" taken acutely, as opposed to a daily 5-minute practice.
- The 2018 Lee & DiGiuseppe anger meta-analysis was not retrieved, and no evidence specific to "check the facts" or "opposite action" was found.

## 4. Habit reversal training (HRT): awareness training, competing responses, and learning to notice early warning signs

### Takeaway
HRT's core active ingredients are generally identified as awareness training (learning to detect the behavior and its early precursors) and a competing response used *contingently*, each time the urge or behavior is detected. This maps closely onto what Steady is trying to do. The evidence comes mainly from tics, hair-pulling and other body-focused habits, not emotional reactions, so applying it here is reasoning by analogy.

### Cited Findings
- Bate, Malouff, Thorsteinsson & Bhullar (2011, *Clinical Psychology Review* 31(5):865–871) is the standard meta-analysis of HRT for tics, habit disorders and stuttering. Its pooled effect sizes were not visible in the snippets. — [Wikipedia citation listing](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Habit_reversal_training)
- (Older, 1995.) Reviews identify awareness training and the competing response as HRT's essential components. — [PubMed 7593685, "Habit reversal: a review of applications and variations"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7593685/)
- A secondary summary citing Miltenberger & Fuqua (older, 1985) reports that only contingent use of the competing response (every time the behavior or urge occurs) helps. Scheduled or random use does not. — [Wikipedia, Habit reversal training](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Habit_reversal_training)
- Recent analyses still treat competing-response training and awareness training as HRT's most effective techniques. — [Springer, Cognitive Therapy and Research 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10608-022-10334-9)

### Inferences
- **Steady as awareness training:** Real-time detection works as an external awareness trainer. The aim is to move detection from the app to the user. Practical steps: (1) after a meeting, show flagged moments and ask what the user noticed in their body or thoughts just *before* the drift (precursors); (2) build a personal list of early warning signs and triggers (topics, people, interruptions); (3) add a "self-catch" tap so the user can log noticing a reaction before the app does. Self-catches *before* a nudge can be a key metric of skill transfer.
- **Competing response:** Each user chooses one discreet, incompatible response, for example a slow exhale plus relaxing the hands, rather than raising the voice and leaning forward. The nudge should cue *that* response every time. Contingent pairing is the active ingredient, so random reminders or "pre-emptive calm" prompts are less likely to work.
- HRT is normally a therapist-delivered package with social support and practice. Steady supplies only the detection and cueing parts.

### Gaps
- No studies were found applying HRT specifically to anger outbursts or emotional reactions in adults at work.
- Bate et al. (2011) effect sizes could not be confirmed. Snippets mentioned large effects, around 0.80 across disorders and ES 1.41 for hair-pulling behavior therapy, but the source pages could not be identified with confidence, so those numbers are left out of the findings.

## 5. Self-monitoring and its reactivity; ecological momentary intervention and JITAIs: when to prompt, receptivity, burden

### Takeaway
Monitoring progress toward a goal does change behavior. A meta-analysis of 138 experiments found d = 0.40 for goal attainment, with larger effects when monitoring is physically recorded or made public. So "reactivity" is useful here, not just noise. The JITAI framework gives Steady a design vocabulary (decision points, tailoring variables, intervention options, decision rules). The best-known micro-randomized trial capped prompts at two a day to limit burden and habituation and found modest effects close to the significance threshold. Prompt burden is consistently reported as a limitation.

### Cited Findings
- Harkin et al. (2016, *Psychological Bulletin* 142(2):198–229) included 138 randomized studies (N = 19,951). Interventions increased how often people monitored (d+ = 1.98, 95% CI 1.71–2.24) and improved goal attainment (d+ = 0.40, 95% CI 0.32–0.48). Changes in monitoring frequency partly mediated the effect. Effects were larger when progress was reported publicly or physically recorded. Monitoring an *outcome* (e.g., weight) changed the outcome but not the related behavior. — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/); [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/progress-goals)
- Assessment reactivity: a 2026 JMIR paper separates "self-report reactivity" from real behavior change. It cites evidence that repeated assessment changes behavior, e.g., older adults were less sedentary in the first 2–3 days of monitoring. — [JMIR 2026 e99953](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e99953)
- A 2021 JMIR mHealth systematic review of smartphone ecological momentary interventions found the field is emerging, with few efficacy evaluations. — [JMIR mHealth 2021 e22890](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2021/11/e22890/)
- The JITAI framework (Nahum-Shani et al., *Annals of Behavioral Medicine* 52(6):446–462, online Dec 2016/2017) has six elements: distal outcome, proximal outcomes, decision points (moments when an intervention decision must be made), intervention options, tailoring variables (individual information used to decide when and how to intervene), and decision rules. — [PMC5364076](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5364076); [NIH OBSSR summary](https://obssr.od.nih.gov/news-and-events/news/how-design-best-just-time-mobile-interventions)
- In the JITAI literature, receptivity is the person's ability to receive, process and use the support provided. A 2025 study of 70 students split it into *acceptance* (engaging with the prompt) and *feasibility* (being able to act on it given the situation). — [arXiv 2508.02817](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.02817)
- HeartSteps micro-randomized trial (Klasnja et al. 2019; 44 adults, 6 weeks): a contextually tailored suggestion, compared with none, increased steps in the next 30 minutes by 14%, about 35 steps (p = .06). Two suggestions a day were chosen "to minimize burden and reduce the risk of habituation". A pre-specified hypothesis held that the effect would decline over time in the study. The result of that analysis was not in the snippets. — [PubMed 30192907](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/); [PMC6401341](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6401341); [trial registry](https://ctv.veeva.com/study/a-micro-randomized-trial-of-heartsteps-an-mhealth-intervention-for-increasing-physical-activity)
- Habituation in micro-randomized trial terms: attention to push stimuli deteriorates until the person may stop noticing them. — [arXiv 2107.03544](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.03544)
- Reviews list burden from frequent prompts as a recurring JITAI challenge, e.g., a 2026 review of dietary JITAIs. A 2025 qualitative systematic review of mental-health JITAIs identifies engagement as a key point to optimize. — [JMIR 2026 e92139](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e92139/PDF); [Frontiers in Digital Health 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/digital-health/articles/10.3389/fdgth.2025.1460167/text)

### Inferences
- Steady already is a JITAI. Mapping it: distal outcome = fewer regretted reactions and more self-regulation without the app. Proximal outcome = recovery within N seconds of a nudge, or a self-catch. Decision points = each detected drift. Tailoring variables = drift size, whether the user is speaking or listening, how many nudges so far in this meeting, time since the last nudge, and the user's stage in the training plan. Intervention options should include *no nudge*.
- Micro-randomized trials are the natural way to test nudge type, timing and fading, because Steady makes many in-meeting decisions per user.
- Receptivity in meetings depends heavily on the situation. A screen-color change may be unreadable while the user is presenting, and a vibration may be fine. "Feasibility" (being able to act without social cost) matters as much as "acceptance".
- Harkin's result that physical recording strengthens monitoring supports a one-tap self-log of a self-catch, and a private post-meeting log. Public sharing is not advisable in a workplace context; that is a judgment call, not tested.

### Gaps
- No JITAI or micro-randomized trial was found that targets emotional reactions in live meetings.
- No sourced optimal nudge rate per hour or per meeting was found. HeartSteps' two per day is a design choice for a different behavior.
- Nahum-Shani's specific guidance on "providing nothing" as an option and on burden could not be confirmed from snippets.

## 6. Fading prompts and scaffolding toward independence: how should feedback frequency change over time?

### Takeaway
Behavior analysis and motor learning both treat dependence on the prompt as a real risk. Prompt fading moves control from the artificial cue to natural cues, and the guidance hypothesis predicts that feedback on every trial helps practice performance but can harm retention. The motor-learning evidence for reduced-frequency feedback is **contested**: an early meta-analysis supports it, and more recent preregistered syntheses do not find robust effects. Fading should therefore be built in and tested, not assumed to work at a fixed schedule.

### Cited Findings
- A systematic review of prompt-fading procedures (45 articles, 46 experiments) found that all prompt-fading procedures were generally effective for acquiring behavior. The authors recommend individualized assessment of stimulus control. A follow-up (Part II) supports self-paced, individualized fading. — [Springer, J. Developmental & Physical Disabilities](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10882-017-9575-8); [Penn State record, Part II](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-review-of-prompt-fading-procedures-part-ii-parameters-and-compo/)
- In behavior analysis the instructor must program transfer of stimulus control from prompts to natural discriminative stimuli. Prompt dependence is when the prompt, not the natural cue, keeps controlling the response. — [Prompt and Prompt-Fading Procedures (2023 handbook chapter)](https://mdsoar.org/handle/11603/28148)
- Guidance hypothesis: feedback after every trial helps during practice but can degrade later learning compared with reduced feedback. (Older, 2007.) Marschall, Bund & Wiemeyer coded 40 studies and reported a "reversal effect": reduced feedback tended to hurt acquisition but improve learning. — [Marschall et al. 2007 (PDF)](https://orbilu.uni.lu/bitstream/10993/4589/1/Publikation_Marschall_Bund_Wiemeyer_2007_EJournal%20Bewegung%20und%20Training.PDF)
- *Contradicting this:* a separate meta-analysis of the reduced relative feedback frequency effect concluded that "robust evidence regarding feedback frequency and motor learning is lacking". A preregistered meta-analysis in the *Journal of Expertise and Motor Skills* found no support for effects of feedback frequency that depend on when retention is tested (immediate vs. delayed). — [AMiner record](https://www.aminer.org/pub/623008115aee126c0fa6459b/meta-analysis-of-the-reduced-relative-feedback-frequency-effect-on-motor-learning); [JEMS](https://www.scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/2529)
- A 2024 postural-control experiment calls the effects of reduced feedback frequency "controversial". In its data, 67% feedback outperformed lower frequencies, so less feedback is not always better. — [PMC10933749](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933749)

### Inferences
- **Recommended trajectory (to be tested, not proven):**
  - Phase 1, *acquisition*: dense, immediate nudges to build awareness, paired with post-meeting review.
  - Phase 2, *bandwidth*: nudge only when drift exceeds a wider band, so small drifts are left for the user to self-detect, and add a "self-catch" button.
  - Phase 3, *self-estimate first*: after a meeting, the user rates their own moments before seeing Steady's data, which builds internal calibration.
  - Phase 4, *summary or delayed*: no live nudges by default, only a post-meeting summary, with live nudges kept for large deviations.
  - Phase 5, *maintenance*: periodic "check-up" meetings with full feedback.
- Fading should move on performance criteria such as self-catch rate and recovery time, individualized as prompt-fading reviews recommend, not on the calendar. It should move back to denser support after relapses or in high-stakes periods.
- Because the motor-learning evidence is mixed, Steady should run its own experiments, e.g., a micro-randomized trial or A/B test of feedback density, measuring behavior on later *no-nudge* meetings, which is the analogue of a retention test, rather than in-meeting performance.
- "Natural cues" for Steady are the person's own early-warning signs (heat, a tight jaw, an urge to interrupt), identified through awareness training in section 4. The goal is for those cues, not the vibration, to trigger the competing response.

### Gaps
- No study was found that applies prompt fading or guidance-hypothesis designs to *emotion-regulation* feedback.
- The self-controlled feedback literature (learners choose when to get feedback) was not retrieved.

## 7. Alert fatigue, reactance, self-criticism vs. self-compassion, and the risk that warnings increase anxiety or self-consciousness

### Takeaway
Feedback is not reliably positive. In a classic meta-analysis it improved performance on average (d = .41) but *reduced* it in more than a third of cases, and its effectiveness fell as feedback drew attention toward the self and away from the task. People habituate quickly to repeated warnings, and warnings that vary in appearance resist habituation. Self-compassion after mistakes increases motivation to improve compared with boosting self-esteem. The evidence that wearable feedback increases anxiety is weak and mixed.

### Cited Findings
- (Older, 1996.) Kluger & DeNisi (*Psychological Bulletin* 119:254–284) analyzed 607 effect sizes (23,663 observations). Feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = .41), but more than one-third *decreased* performance. Effectiveness fell as attention moved up the hierarchy toward the self (meta-task processes) and away from task details. — [Kluger & DeNisi abstract (HUJI)](https://pluto.huji.ac.il/%7Emskluger/files/Download/Kluger%20&%20%20DeNisi%20abstract.doc); [HUJI record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/)
- Habituation to warnings (Vance, Anderson, Kirwan, Jenkins et al.): in a 5-day fMRI and eye-tracking study, attention to repeated warnings fell sharply across the week with partial rebound between days. "Polymorphic" warnings, which vary their appearance, resisted habituation much better. In a field study, people who received polymorphic warnings kept higher adherence at 3 weeks. The authors' advice to designers: use fewer warnings and add novelty. — [BYU ScholarsArchive (CHI 2017)](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/9293); [MIS Quarterly 2018](https://aisel.aisnet.org/misq/vol42/iss2/3); [BYU Marriott news](https://marriott.byu.edu/infosys/news/article?id=1412)
- Neural habituation shows widespread linear drops in activation with repeated exposure and a rise in default-mode activity, interpreted as mind-wandering. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.528079/pdf)
- (Older, 2012.) Breines & Chen (*Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*), four experiments: inducing self-compassion about weaknesses, failures and past moral transgressions increased motivation to improve, try harder and avoid repeating mistakes, compared with a self-esteem induction or a positive-mood control. Press coverage reports that the self-compassion group studied 33% longer than the self-esteem group and 51% longer than controls after a failed test. — [PubMed 22645164](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22645164/); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-self-compassion-solution/)
- Self-compassion intervention meta-analysis (Ferrari et al. 2019, *Mindfulness*; 27 RCTs): improvements across 11 psychosocial outcomes, large for rumination (g = 1.37). A secondary listing reports self-criticism g = 0.56, which was not confirmed against the original. Intervention types were too diverse to compare, and publication bias may be present. — [ACBS record](https://contextualscience.org/publications/ferrari_hunt_harrysunker_abbott_beath_einstein_2019); [ACU research bank](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/87545/self-compassion-interventions-and-psychosocial-outcomes-a-meta-analysis-of-rcts)
- A more conservative 2023 update, apparently Han & Kim in *Mindfulness*, found small-to-medium effects on depression, anxiety and stress at post-test. It argues that the 2019 review included interventions that had only a self-compassion component. — [Springer Mindfulness 2023](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12671-023-02148-x.pdf)
- Anxiety from wearable feedback: evidence is weak and mixed. Small studies of heart patients and of people with atrial fibrillation linked biometric tracking to more symptom-checking and anxiety (reported in popular press). A 2025 student thesis (n = 70, within-subject) found no increase in perceived stress or health anxiety from wearable stress feedback in healthy people. False *slow*-heart-rate feedback through a wearable (EmotionCheck, 2017, n = 67) *reduced* anxiety. — [Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/wearables/psychological-drawback-fitness-trackers/); [U. Twente thesis](https://essay.utwente.nl/essays/107074); [Cornell EmotionCheck](https://interplay.infosci.cornell.edu/emotioncheck)

### Inferences
- **Nudge tone:** Keep nudges task-focused and neutral, e.g., "Pause. Exhale." Avoid nudges about the self or that judge ("You're losing it", red alarm colors, "Warning"). Kluger & DeNisi predict that self-focused feedback is most likely to backfire, and that risk is higher for users already prone to shame or social anxiety.
- **Habituation management:** cap nudges per meeting, use a *small* set of variations in modality and pattern (polymorphism), and reserve the strongest signal for the largest deviations. There is a trade-off: variety reduces habituation but could add distraction or startle. Test both.
- **After-meeting framing:** Use self-compassionate, normalizing language ("Tough meeting. You recovered in 40 seconds at minute 12"). Celebrate self-catches and recoveries rather than counting "incidents". Avoid streaks that punish a lapse.
- Steady's on-screen color change could be visible to others and cause embarrassment. Discreet channels (haptic, a peripheral glow) lower the risk of self-consciousness. This is an inference; no direct study was found.
- Give users control over nudge modality, intensity and pausing. Perceived autonomy is likely to reduce reactance (inference; see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No sourced study was retrieved on psychological reactance to health or behavior-change messages (e.g., Rains 2013 meta-analysis), so reactance claims here are inference.
- No direct evidence was found that real-time emotion or behavior nudges increase self-focused attention or social anxiety. Clark & Wells-type models predict it, but they were not sourced in this pass.
- No clinical-grade alert-fatigue literature, e.g., from EHR medication alerts, was retrieved.

## 8. Biofeedback and wearable or app-based stress and emotion-regulation interventions: what the evidence shows

### Takeaway
HRV biofeedback has a moderate-to-large pooled effect on self-reported stress and anxiety in an older meta-analysis (g ≈ 0.8, 24 studies, about 484 people), but later reviews report smaller or mixed effects and small trials. Mental-health apps produce small effects (g ≈ 0.26–0.28 for depression and anxiety across 176 RCTs), inflated when compared with inactive controls ("digital placebo"). The effect of commercial wearables on stress has barely been tested.

### Cited Findings
- Goessl, Curtiss & Hofmann (2017, *Psychological Medicine*): 24 studies, 484 participants, pre-post g = 0.81 for HRV biofeedback on stress and anxiety. Efficacy was not moderated by study year, risk of bias, number of sessions or presence of an anxiety disorder. The authors call for better-controlled studies and note the promise of wearables. — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/effect-of-heart-rate-variability-biofeedback-training-on-stress-and-anxiety-a-metaanalysis/A839E9C968E54774DF5C8FB186764EF0); [BU OpenBU](https://open.bu.edu/handle/2144/26911)
- Lehrer et al. (2020), as summarized in later literature, report small-to-medium effects of HRV biofeedback across anxiety, depression, anger and performance, and evidence on stress is described as "more mixed". This is from snippets only. — [Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10484-025-09750-w)
- A 2025 JMIR systematic review of workplace biofeedback (studies 2012–2024) found promising *short-term* reductions in stress, particularly with breathing techniques and mobile delivery, but small samples and limited long-term follow-up. — [JMIR 2025 e70134](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e70134)
- A 2023 scoping review of wearables for stress management found 21 studies using commercial devices, mostly smartwatches and bands, and noted that their effect on reducing stress had not yet been analyzed. — [PMC10486660](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10486660/)
- Linardon et al. (2024, *World Psychiatry* 23(1):139–149), 176 RCTs: apps vs. controls had depression g = 0.28 (NNT ≈ 11.5) and generalized anxiety g = 0.26 (NNT ≈ 12.4). Effects were robust to removing small or high-risk-of-bias trials. Effects were larger for CBT-based apps and chatbots (depression) and for CBT or mood-monitoring apps (anxiety). Social anxiety g = 0.52, OCD g = 0.51, PTSD g = 0.12. Inactive controls inflated effects (a possible "digital placebo"), and heterogeneity was substantial. — [PMC10785982](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10785982); [Mental Elf summary](https://www.nationalelfservice.net/mental-health/depression/apps-for-depression-and-anxiety-big-new-meta-analysis-supports-effectiveness/)

### Inferences
- HRV or resonance-breathing practice *between* meetings (a few minutes a day) is the most evidence-aligned "training" component Steady could add. The in-meeting nudge then cues a breath pattern the user has already practiced, which fits the Balban finding that benefits grew with days of practice.
- Expect small average effects at the population level. Avoid efficacy claims beyond what Steady's own trials show. Use active comparators, e.g., a version without live nudges, to avoid "digital placebo" inflation.
- Mood-monitoring features were associated with larger anxiety effects in Linardon 2024, which supports the value of the self-monitoring component itself.

### Gaps
- No 2024–2026 meta-analysis specific to smartwatches or real-time wearable feedback for stress was found.
- No trial was found of *real-time* face, voice or language emotion feedback in meetings. Steady's core modality appears untested in the peer-reviewed literature, based on this search.

## 9. Reflection and review between events: post-event review, implementation intentions, trigger identification, spaced practice

### Takeaway
Structured debriefs reliably improve performance (d ≈ 0.67–0.79), especially with objective performance data and the right alignment. Implementation intentions (if-then plans) have a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65) and a large effect on emotional outcomes. Spaced practice improves retention in general learning. Together these support building the skill *between* meetings, with Steady's in-meeting data feeding review and planning.

### Cited Findings
- (Older, 2013.) Tannenbaum & Cerasoli's meta-analysis of team and individual debriefs found an improvement of about 25% over controls (d = .67). — [PubMed 23516804](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23516804/)
- Keiser & Arthur's meta-analysis of after-action reviews (61 studies, 107 effect sizes, 915 teams and 3,499 individuals): overall d = 0.79. Two features consistently increased effectiveness: (a) alignment of the review to the individual or the team, and (b) *objective performance-review media*. Summaries say individual reviews work better when led by a content expert and team reviews when self-led. — [scite author page](https://scite.ai/authors/nathanael-l-keiser-JazJ5D); [QIC-WD summary](https://qic-wd.org/node/596); [Keiser & Arthur 2022 record](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/meta-analysis-task-and-training-characteristics-that-contribute-attenuate-the-effectiveness-the-after-action-review-debrief-keiser-nathanael/10013438768)
- (Older, 2006.) Gollwitzer & Sheeran (*Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38:69–119), 94 studies, more than 8,000 participants: implementation intentions improved goal attainment, d = 0.65, mostly *beyond* goal intentions alone. They helped start goal striving, shield goals from unwanted influences (d ≈ .77 for preventing derailment), and disengage from failing actions. Mechanisms: the specified cue becomes more accessible and the response becomes more automatic. The figure is the authors' own meta-analysis. — [Konstanz record](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes); [Frontiers 2015 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00395/full)
- (Older, 2012.) Webb, Schweiger Gallo et al. (*European Review of Social Psychology*) report that implementation intentions modify emotional outcomes, with a large effect compared with no regulation instructions. — [Webb, Schweiger Gallo et al. 2012 (PDF)](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/webb_schweiger_gallo_ea_2012.pdf)
- (Older, 2006.) Cepeda et al. (*Psychological Bulletin* 132(3):354–380), 839 assessments in 317 experiments of verbal recall: spacing improves retention, and the best gap between study sessions grows with how long the material must be retained. Earlier reviews found benefits for motor skills but mixed results for intellectual skills. — [PubMed 16719566](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/); [author PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf)
- Transfer of reappraisal training to daily life is limited and inconsistent. Denny & Ochsner (2014) found that only participants trained in *distancing* reappraisal reported less perceived stress in daily life. A COVID-era written reappraisal training reduced immediate distress, but the advantage disappeared at 2 months. A registered trial (NCT05784831, n = 146) is testing a 21-day reappraisal app against spaced 7-day "bursts" and an EMA-only control; results were not found. — [PMC4096123](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4096123); [COVID reappraisal study (PDF)](https://typeset.io/pdf/reappraising-negative-emotions-reduces-distress-during-the-2l56aeyi.pdf); [trial registry](https://ctv.veeva.com/study/development-and-feasibility-test-of-an-ecological-momentary-intervention-emi-reapp-to-increase)

### Inferences
- **Post-meeting micro-review (2–3 minutes):** show 1–3 flagged moments with objective data (timeline, drift graph). Ask: What triggered it? What did you notice first? What did you do? What would you try next time? This follows the after-action review evidence that objective review media and individual alignment matter.
- **Turn the review into if-then plans:** "If [person] interrupts me while I'm presenting, then I'll exhale slowly and say 'Let me finish this point.'" Steady can surface the relevant plan before a meeting with the same attendees or topic (situation selection and modification in Gross's terms) and cue its *then*-response live.
- **Spaced rehearsal:** short, spaced practice of the competing response and breathing between meetings, plus pre-meeting rehearsal for high-risk meetings. Because the best gap grows with the retention goal, practice reminders can be spaced further apart as skill consolidates.
- Distancing-style reappraisal ("How would I see this in a week?", "What would I tell a colleague?") may transfer better than other reappraisal tactics, but this rests on one study.

### Gaps
- No studies were found on post-event review specifically for emotional reactions at work, as opposed to team task performance.
- Independent replications of the implementation-intention effect size were not retrieved, and the authors' figure may overstate it.

## 10. Populations (ADHD, BPD, PTSD, anxiety): accommodations, cautions, and the boundary between coaching and therapy

### Takeaway
Emotional dysregulation is common in adult ADHD: in one study 55% of adults with ADHD reported deficient emotional self-regulation more severe than 95% of controls. Dysregulation is central to BPD, where DBT skills use is a documented mechanism of change. App effects for PTSD symptoms are very small (g = 0.12). Harms are under-measured in digital mental health trials; about 6.7% of app users deteriorate, similar to controls. The FDA's January 2026 general-wellness guidance covers "stress management" claims but not claims tied to diagnosing or treating a disease or condition.

### Cited Findings
- (Older, 2014.) Shaw, Stringaris, Nigg & Leibenluft (*American Journal of Psychiatry* 171(3):276–293) review emotion dysregulation in ADHD: prevalence, pathophysiology and treatment. — [PubMed 24480998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24480998/)
- (Older, 2013.) Surman, Biederman, Spencer, Miller, McDermott & Faraone: 55% of adults with ADHD reported deficient emotional self-regulation (DESR) more severe than 95% of controls. — [PMC4282137](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282137)
- A 2023 systematic review (Soler-Gutiérrez et al.) found that adults with ADHD consistently show more emotion dysregulation than healthy controls, with medium-to-large effects, supporting it as a core feature of adult ADHD. — [PMC9821724](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9821724/)
- BPD: DBT frames BPD as a disorder of emotion dysregulation stemming from skill deficits. Skills use mediated clinical improvement (older, 2010). — [PMC2914145](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2914145)
- Effects of apps by condition (Linardon 2024): PTSD symptoms g = 0.12 (small), social anxiety g = 0.52, generalized anxiety g = 0.26. — [PMC10785982](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10785982)
- Harms in mental-health app trials (npj Digital Medicine 2024): only a minority of trials reported adverse events. Pooled deterioration was 6.7% (95% CI 4.3–10.1) across 13 app conditions, with no difference from controls. — [DOAJ record, npj Digital Medicine Dec 2024](https://doaj.org/article/56646abb0df94bc2b0fd9c7cc6828cc5)
- A 2023 JMIR Mental Health safety review (23 studies): symptom deterioration was the main treatment-related adverse event. 35% of studies collected no safety data at all, and the main risk mitigation was excluding high-risk groups from trials. — [JMIR Mental Health 2023 e47433](https://mental.jmir.org/2023/1/e47433)
- Monitoring can raise anxiety in clinical groups: in small studies of heart patients and atrial fibrillation patients, tracking was linked to more symptom-checking and anxiety. This comes from popular press and weak evidence. — [Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/wearables/psychological-drawback-fitness-trackers/)
- Regulatory scope: the FDA reissued "General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices" on Jan 6, 2026, superseding the 2019 version. "Relaxation or stress management" and "mental acuity" are listed as general-wellness intended uses. Products may show baselines, trends and summaries in relation to stress and similar domains. Claims that reference a disease or condition fall outside the policy, and how a product is marketed affects its status. Validation expectations for stress claims remain unclear, per law-firm commentary. — [FDA guidance PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/90652/download); [Ropes & Gray alert](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/01/fda-adapts-with-the-times-on-digital-health-updated-guidances-on-general-wellness-products); [Womble Bond Dickinson](https://www.womblebonddickinson.com/us/insights/blogs/fdas-2026-general-wellness-policy-and-what-it-means-manufacturers-wearable-devices)
- Linehan's manual cautions that distress-tolerance (crisis) skills can be overused and should be balanced with problem-solving. — [PositivePsychology.com summary](https://positivepsychology.com/?p=128627)

### Inferences
- **ADHD:** External real-time cues may be especially useful here, since internal self-monitoring is a known weakness. ADHD users may also habituate faster or find nudges distracting. Consider fewer, salient, varied nudges, a slower fading schedule, and strong post-meeting summaries rather than multi-step in-meeting instructions.
- **BPD and high rejection sensitivity:** Language that sounds critical or a flood of "drift" alerts may feel invalidating. Use validating, self-compassionate framing and let the user set sensitivity. Point users to DBT skills groups for deeper skill building rather than having Steady attempt a DBT program.
- **PTSD or trauma histories:** Sudden vibrations or chimes may startle, and attention drawn to bodily arousal may worsen distress for some (inference). Offer gentle or visual-only modes and easy opt-out. App effects for PTSD are small, so Steady should not position itself as relevant to trauma treatment.
- **Anxiety (especially social anxiety):** Constant self-monitoring cues could increase self-focused attention. Bandwidth-only nudges and post-meeting-only modes may suit these users better (inference; see Gaps).
- **Scope guardrails:** Market and design Steady as workplace communication and stress-management coaching, not as diagnosis or treatment of ADHD, BPD, PTSD or anxiety. That keeps it within the FDA wellness framing and is honest about the evidence. Do not give clinical labels to users' patterns.
- **Escalation paths:** Include a clear "this isn't therapy" statement and signposting to professional help (DBT, CBT for anger, ADHD care). Provide crisis resources appropriate to the user's region, to be verified locally. Language that suggests crisis or self-harm should route to crisis resources, not coaching. Track deterioration (e.g., rising self-reported distress or shame) as a safety signal, because trials often fail to measure harm.

### Gaps
- No sourced evidence was retrieved on how people with PTSD or panic disorder respond to interoceptive or arousal-feedback cues. The cautions above are inference.
- No study was found that tests real-time emotion feedback specifically in adults with ADHD or BPD.
- Prevalence of emotional dysregulation in the general workforce was not found.
- Self-focused attention models of social anxiety (Clark & Wells) were not sourced in this pass.
- Rules outside the US (e.g., EU MDR or the EU AI Act's treatment of emotion recognition in workplaces) were not researched here and may be material for a workplace emotion-sensing product.
