# Train the pause, not the poker face

Emotions show up in the face, voice, body and words, but none of these channels reliably reveals what a person feels: people scowl in only about a quarter of their angry moments, microexpressions make up roughly 2% of expressions and are no more common in liars, the voice mostly signals intensity rather than which emotion lies behind it, and single body "tells" such as crossed arms are largely myth. The evidence is much stronger about how signals land, because listeners take meaning mainly from words, pick up feelings through faces and voices, judge within seconds, weigh negative signals more heavily than positive ones, and even blame a person for a laggy connection. In white-collar work, contempt, sarcasm, anger aimed at a person, rudeness and dominating the floor all carry penalties; anger can raise how competent someone looks but costs warmth, the most recent replication finds it lowers status outright, and women, Black women and autistic people pay more for the same behavior. Read against the science of emotion regulation, this means Steady watches roughly the right behaviors but coaches the wrong response: its live nudges ("Soften your face", "Reset to neutral") coach people to hide the expression, a strategy linked to worse memory, strained rapport and burnout, and its 0-to-100 score rewards a still face rather than the user's growing ability to regulate without the app. The changes worth the most are to cue a practiced breathing-and-pause response instead of a fix to the face, measure recovery and self-catches instead of calm, add a two-minute post-meeting review that ends in an if-then plan, make live cues private and rare, and reduce support as the user starts catching reactions before the app does. Nearly every source behind this report was checked only through abstracts and search summaries, not full texts, so effect sizes need rechecking before they appear in public material.

## A scowl accompanies anger only about a quarter of the time

Two questions are easy to confuse: what a signal reveals about the sender's inner state, and how it lands with the people receiving it. Across almost every channel, the evidence on the first is weak and the evidence on the second is much stronger. That split shapes everything else in this report, including what Steady can honestly claim.

### Faces signal social intent better than they reveal feeling

The Facial Action Coding System describes faces as anatomical muscle movements. Basic emotion theory then pairs particular combinations of those movements with emotions, such as the lowered-brow scowl with anger. The landmark 2019 review by Barrett and colleagues tested those pairings against four criteria (reliability, specificity, generalizability and validity). It concluded that facial configurations are **not reliable or specific enough across contexts, people and cultures to diagnose any emotional state** ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/emotional-expressions-reconsidered-challenges-to-inferring-emotion-from-human-facial-movements.html)). By the authors' own summary, **people scowl when angry only about 25% of the time** on average, they scowl for plenty of other reasons, and smiles can signal submission rather than happiness ([Waisman Center](https://www.waisman.wisc.edu/2019/07/24/emotion-detection-applications-built-on-outdated-science-report-warns/)). A 2021 follow-up analyzed 604 photographs of professional actors portraying emotion-evoking scenarios. It found variable configurations, with only fear, happiness and surprise portrayed even moderately reliably, and observers' ratings of the same face shifted once they saw the scenario ([Nature Communications](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41467-021-25352-6)).

The strongest counterevidence comes from Cowen, Keltner and colleagues. They used a neural network to classify 16 expression types in about 6 million YouTube videos from 144 countries and found that **about 70% of context–expression associations were shared across regions** ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-3037-7)). That classifier learned from human judgments of expressions, though. So the study shows that *perceived* expressions occur in similar situations worldwide, not that a face reliably indicates a felt emotion. The behavioral-ecology view offers a bridge between the camps: facial displays are tools of social influence, and in Fridlund's words, "our faces are not about us, but about where we want a social interaction to go" ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/radical-new-theory-proposes-facial-expressions-are-not-emotional-displays-tools)). Both sides end up supporting the same practical claim. A face tells you a good deal about how a moment will be read and where an interaction is heading, and little about what the person feels inside.

How people perceive faces is also the better-documented half of the story. Even neutral faces that structurally resemble anger are judged less trustworthy and more hostile. These impressions "are not necessarily accurate" yet "affect important social outcomes" ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3778026)). Contempt is the only expression that is unilateral, or one-sided ([Paul Ekman Group](https://paulekman.com/universal-emotions/what-is-contempt)). The Gottman Institute describes eye rolls as contemptuous gestures that people often fail to notice in themselves ([Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/blog/what-causes-contempt-in-relationships)). The movements Steady tracks (brow lowering, lip pressing, nose wrinkling, a one-sided smile, an upward eye roll) are therefore defensible signals of *how a moment may land*, as long as they are never presented as readings of emotion.

Machines add their own error on top. MediaPipe's 52 blendshapes only "loosely correspond" to facial action units ([py-feat](https://huggingface.co/py-feat/mp_blendshapes)). The first published mapping between the two (Turrisi et al., 2026) rests on the consensus of ten clinical psychologists, with 98% of mappings getting majority support, and the accessible summaries report no validation against FACS-coded ground truth ([IRIS CNR](https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/583461)). In a test of eight commercial emotion classifiers on 937 dynamic videos, **accuracy ranged from 48% to 62% against about 72% for humans**, and accuracy was lower on spontaneous expressions than on posed ones ([PLOS ONE](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7182192/)). One commercial system scored Black NBA players as angrier than white players even after controlling for smiling ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3281765)). Automated action-unit detection also degrades as the head turns away from the camera; earlier work found it robust only to about 15 degrees ([Behavior Research Methods](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13428-014-0536-1)). Individual differences are large as well. Autistic people produce facial expressions less often and more briefly (summary effect −0.48) ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30393953/)), and people with Parkinsonian facial masking are judged less trustworthy, with a larger penalty for women ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5003752)).

### Microexpressions are real, rare and useless for catching lies

Microexpressions do occur, but in the main high-stakes coding study of 700 genuine and faked emotional expressions **only about 2% were microexpressions, and they appeared about equally in honest and deceptive people** ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6158306)). Burgoon notes that most liars show none at all ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01672)). The commercial Micro Expression Training Tool improves recognition of brief faces on tests. In a controlled trial, though, trainees detected lies no better than people given bogus training or none, and overall accuracy was slightly below chance ([Jordan et al. 2019](https://web.williams.edu/Psychology/Faculty/Kassin/files/Jordan%20et%20al%20(2019)%20-%20METT.pdf)). The US Government Accountability Office found no valid evidence that the TSA's behavior-detection indicators identified threats, and TSA ended the program in fiscal 2017 ([GAO-14-159](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-14-159)). Microexpressions are also technically out of reach for Steady. A fleeting facial event spans only a handful of webcam frames, where blendshape values are noisy. Steady's eye-roll cue already fires after a 0.25-second hold and its smirk cue after 0.4 seconds, which is close to that territory. Steady should never claim to detect microexpressions, "hidden" feelings or dishonesty.

### The voice measures intensity, and cold anger slips past it

The voice reliably carries **arousal**. Anger, fear and happiness generally go with higher and more variable pitch and a faster rate, while sadness lowers pitch, rate and intensity ([Pell et al. 2009](https://www.mcgill.ca/pell_lab/files/pell_lab/pell_paulmann_dara_alasseri__kotz_2009_0.pdf)). Not every hostile stance runs hot, though. Hot anger raises mean pitch and pitch range, while **cold anger lowers mean pitch with no change in range** ([ICPhS 2019](https://assta.org/proceedings/ICPhS2019Microsite/pdf/full-paper_599.pdf)). Sarcasm is marked by a slower tempo and lower pitch ([Rockwell 2000](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1005120109296); [Cheang & Pell 2008](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~julia/papers/cheangDell08.pdf)), and slowness is its most consistent marker across languages ([Lan & Mok 2025](https://ling.cuhk.edu.hk/people/peggy/LanMok_L&S_Sarcasm_2025.pdf)). A 2026 study using synthetic speech found that loudness combined with a flat pitch contour was the most reliable sarcasm cue, which partly conflicts with the older work ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.09717)). Findings on the anxious voice are contradictory even about the direction of pitch and pause effects ([Laukka et al. 2008](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10919-008-0055-9); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.08534)).

Two further facts matter for Steady. First, people differ a lot. Switching from speaker-independent to speaker-dependent models improves recognition of *valence* (how positive or negative someone sounds) by up to 30%, but improves arousal by less than 4% ([Sridhar & Busso](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2201.07876)). Transformer models turned out fair across sex groups but **not across individual speakers** ([Wagner et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07378)). Second, the environment mimics emotion. In background noise, speakers involuntarily raise their loudness and pitch and shift energy toward higher frequencies, which is the Lombard effect ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5398925/); [Lu & Cooke 2008](https://personal.utdallas.edu/~hxb076000/citing_papers/Lu_Speech%20Production%20Modifications%20Produced%20by%20Competing%20Talkers.pdf)). That is exactly the "louder and sharper" pattern Steady treats as a raised voice. Steady's voice features therefore work as an arousal meter. They cannot tell anger from enthusiasm, they will mostly miss cold anger and contempt, and they will fire in a noisy café or on a call where the user struggles to hear. They cannot support labels such as "May sound angry", "May sound stressed" or "May sound anxious".

### The body offers few reliable tells, and a webcam sees little of it

An analysis of more than 1,300 estimates for 158 commonly cited deception cues, including fidgeting, gaze aversion and face touching, found weak or no links to lying. People detect lies at roughly 54% accuracy, barely above chance ([Earth.com summary](https://www.earth.com/news/scientists-set-the-record-straight-by-clarifying-common-myths-about-body-language)). The link between crossed arms and defensiveness rests on sparse evidence ([CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/74373521.pdf)). A large replication of power posing found no effect on hormones or behavior, only on how powerful people said they felt ([Ranehill et al. 2015](https://www.zne.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:e5fc4c3c-d50e-4aa9-96ab-4389e426d20d/Psychological%20Science-2015-Ranehill-0956797614553946.pdf)). The best-supported body cue is self-touch, which rises with *momentary* anxiety (r = .33 in one sample of 42 men) ([University of Aberdeen](https://aura.abdn.ac.uk/items/5147ea47-479b-4e75-8326-c672a2dc9109/full)). Researchers caution that self-touch may itself be a way of calming down ([J. Nonverbal Behavior](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10919-022-00402-9)). A laptop camera mostly frames the head and shoulders, and on video, "eye contact" depends on where the camera sits: perceived eye contact peaks when the speaker looks about 2 degrees *below* the lens ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.17104)). A camera-based coach gains little from body cues, and posture or eye-contact scores would mislead.

### Words carry the clearest signal of hostile stance

Most of the evidence on hostile language comes from couples research. Gottman's "Four Horsemen" are criticism, contempt (sarcasm, mockery, eye-rolling), defensiveness and stonewalling ([Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-four-horsemen-contempt-criticism-defensiveness-and-stonewalling/)). The institute's claims of more than 90% accuracy in predicting divorce are self-reported, and an independent review even found contempt associated with *lower* serious consideration of divorce ([Johnson 2002](https://www.binghamton.edu/psychology/labs/marriage/documents/johnson_2002.pdf)). Better-controlled studies support narrower claims. In 59 couples, "you" language during problem-solving went with negativity and blame, and "I" language went with better outcomes ([Simmons et al. 2005](https://petercgordon.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/17516/2018/08/SimmonsGordonChamblessPsychSci2005.pdf)). When people rated conflict openers, **"I" language that also stated the other person's perspective was rated least hostile, and a "you" statement with no perspective was most likely to provoke defensiveness** ([Rogers et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5961625)). Absolutist words ("always", "nothing", "completely") track the severity of anxiety and depression better than negative-emotion words do ([APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/all-or-nothing-thinking-more-common-in-people-with-anxiety-depression-and-suicidal-ideation.html)). That means "I always mess this up" signals distress, not hostility. No validated lexicon of confrontational *workplace* phrases exists.

| Channel | What it reliably carries | What it cannot reveal | Implication for Steady |
|---|---|---|---|
| Face | How a moment is likely to be read; social intent | Felt emotion, lies, "hidden" feelings, microexpressions | Keep movement cues framed as how they may land; never name emotions |
| Voice | Arousal and intensity (loudness, pitch, pace, sharpness) | Which emotion; cold anger, contempt and sarcasm run low and slow | Label as intensity; correct for noise; rely on words for stance |
| Body | Modest link between self-touch and momentary anxiety | Defensiveness from posture; lying from fidgeting | Little worth adding; avoid posture and eye-contact scores |
| Words | Blame, contempt, defensiveness, dismissal, repair | Sarcasm from text alone | Main signal of stance; refine the phrase list |

## Listeners read words for meaning but catch feelings from faces and voices

The popular "7-38-55" rule (7% words, 38% tone, 55% face) comes from two small 1967 studies of single words and photographs. Mehrabian himself says it does not apply beyond feelings conveyed through inconsistent cues ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Mehrabian); [Lapakko 2007](https://core.ac.uk/download/214121529.pdf)). When perceivers try to work out what someone is actually thinking or feeling, **verbal content contributes most to accuracy, then the voice, then visual cues**. When people are asked specifically about *feelings*, they lean more on what they see ([Hall & Schmid Mast 2007](https://serval.unil.ch/notice/serval:BIB_2D283015F24F); [Zaki et al. 2009](https://scan.psych.columbia.edu/papers/Zaki_et_al_2009.pdf)). Emotions also spread through automatic mimicry of faces, voices and posture ([Hatfield et al.](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/1467-8721.ep10770953/)). In managerial decision groups, one confederate's mood spread to the others, and positive contagion produced more cooperation and less conflict ([Barsade 2002](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/2002/barsade2002a.pdf)). A tense face or sharp voice can therefore change how colleagues *feel* even when the words are fine. That is a sound reason for a coach to watch more than words.

Van Kleef's Emotions as Social Information (EASI) model explains what observers do next. They draw inferences from a display ("they're near their limit") and they also react emotionally ("I feel attacked"). Anger wins concessions in negotiation only under narrow conditions: it targets the offer rather than the person, and the observer is in a weak position ([EASI chapter](https://dare.uva.nl/document/2/61635)). Anger pays off in disputes over interests and backfires in disputes over values ([Negotiation Journal](https://direct.mit.edu/ngtn/article-pdf/30/4/455/2379468/nejo12071.pdf)). A 2020 meta-analysis of 64 studies found that **negative displays help claim value but diminish trust** ([Sharma et al. 2020](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-effects-of-emotional-expressions-in-negotiation-a-meta-analys/)), and the gains from anger come with a cost in covert retaliation ([ANU](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/beyond-negotiated-outcomes-the-hidden-costs-of-anger-expression-i/)). Most meetings are repeated relationships rather than one-shot negotiations, so the trust costs almost certainly dominate. Sarcasm raises conflict for both sender and receiver unless the two already trust each other ([Huang, Gino & Galinsky 2015](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/55dcde36e4b0df55a96ab220/t/569e634a05f8e2b24db420a4/1453220682892/Huang+Gino+Galinsky+OBHDP+2015.pdf)). That gives a coach good grounds to warn more strongly about hostility aimed at a person than about frustration aimed at an issue.

Judgments form fast and lean negative. Strangers watching **silent clips under 30 seconds** predicted teachers' end-of-semester evaluations ([Ambady & Rosenthal 1993](https://www.math.stonybrook.edu/~bishop/classes/math598.F25/Half_a_Minute.pdf)), and about 100 milliseconds of a face yields trait judgments close to those made with unlimited time ([Lumen textbook summary](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-social-psychology/chapter/initial-impression-formation/)). Bad impressions form faster and resist disconfirmation more than good ones ([Baumeister et al. 2001](https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Baumeister_2001.pdf)), though this negativity bias is "far from universal" ([Rozin & Royzman 2001](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/2001/rozin2001a.pdf)). In 124 newlywed couples, coding just the **first three minutes** of a conflict discussion predicted divorce over six years ([Carrère & Gottman 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10526767/)). Together these findings suggest that the opening of a hard conversation carries outsized weight, and that one contemptuous moment can outweigh several warm ones.

Speakers misjudge themselves too. People overestimate how visible their inner states are, the illusion of transparency ([Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1998/the-illusion-of-transparency-biased-assessments-of-others-ability/?p=1)), and how much others notice them, the spotlight effect ([Gilovich et al. 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10707330/)). In one secondary report, simply telling speakers about the illusion lowered their stress and improved how their speeches were rated ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_transparency)). Observers bring their own distortions. Higher implicit prejudice predicts seeing anger more readily in Black faces ([Hugenberg & Bodenhausen 2003](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2003/facing-prejudice-implicit-prejudice-and-the-perception-of-facial/?p=1)). Non-autistic observers form negative thin-slice impressions of autistic people, and **the bias disappears when they judge transcripts**, so it is driven by delivery style rather than substance ([Sasson et al. 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep40700)). Under the "double empathy" account, such misunderstanding runs both ways rather than reflecting a deficit on one side ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/autism-human-connection-and-the-double-empathy-problem)).

### Video calls make the network look like a personality flaw

Latency of just 30–70 milliseconds disrupts the rhythm of turn-taking, according to press coverage of Boland and colleagues ([University of Michigan](https://news.umich.edu/zoom-disrupts-the-rhythm-of-conversation)). In clinical video consultations, delay produced silences and overlapping talk ([Seuren et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7819463)). With 1.2-second delays, listeners rated their partners **less attentive and less conscientious, while rating call quality as unaffected**. They blamed the person, not the connection ([Schoenenberg et al. 2014](https://researchr.org/publication/SchoenenbergRK14)). Bailenson's "nonverbal overload" account of Zoom fatigue started as theory ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2021/02/four-causes-zoom-fatigue-solutions)). A survey of 9,787 people later supported it, finding women more fatigued, with "mirror anxiety" as a key mediator ([Fauville et al. 2023](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/videoconferencing/video-conferencing-usage-dynamics-and-nonverbal-mechanisms)). Looking at one's self-view while listening raised negative affect by increasing public self-awareness ([BISE 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12599-024-00874-7)). A 2025 study, however, found mirror anxiety unrelated to fatigue ([Cyberpsychology](https://cyberpsychology.eu/article/view/40292)), so this mechanism is not settled. For Steady, the implications are concrete. A live screen with a cartoon face mirroring the user is effectively a second self-view. Pauses and talk-overs on a call are often network artifacts that a coach should not blame on the user.

## At work, anger trades warmth for a shrinking status bonus

The classic finding is that expressing anger rather than sadness earned a job applicant more status, mediated by higher perceived competence ([Tiedens 2001](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Affect/Tiedens.pdf)). Angry expressers were nonetheless rated colder and less likable, and a 2024 study that copied the original paradigms more exactly found that **angry workers were given less status than sad or emotionless ones** ([Frontiers in Social Psychology 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/social-psychology/articles/10.3389/frsps.2024.1337715/full)). Brescoll and Uhlmann found the status bonus went only to men. Angry female professionals lost status because observers read their anger as a trait, and **the penalty disappeared when the woman gave an external reason for her anger** ([Brescoll & Uhlmann 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18315800/)). The later record conflicts. A conceptual replication with 1,095 participants, run during the #MeToo era, found angry women rated *more* favorably ([McCormick-Huhn & Shields 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7947812)). Two experiments (n = 555), on the other hand, found that Black women's anger was attributed to internal traits, which lowered their performance and leadership ratings ([Motro et al. 2022](https://www.ethicalpsychology.com/2022/04/race-and-reactions-to-womens.html)). The defensible position is that anger at work is a net-risk display for everyone and reliably riskier for some, and that the shared mechanism is whether observers attribute it to the situation or to the person.

Other displays follow the same attribution logic. Men who cried while receiving negative feedback were rated lower on competence and leadership fitness, while women showed no significant penalty ([BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/men-study-suggests-its-really-bad-idea-cry-front-your-colleagues)). Upset people who described themselves as "passionate" rather than "emotional" were judged more competent ([HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/the-right-way-to-cry-in-front-of-your-boss)). A heavy foreign accent lowered how truthful statements seemed, but a mild accent did not, and replications are mixed ([Lev-Ari & Keysar 2010](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_1837436_6/component/file_1837698/LevAriKeysar.pdf); [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10885845/)). That is reason enough for Steady never to score "clarity" or fluency.

The best-measured costs fall on *other people*. Among people treated rudely at work, **48% deliberately cut their effort and 80% lost work time worrying** ([McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/people-and-organization/our-insights/the-hidden-toll-of-workplace-incivility)). In a separate survey, 98% of workers reported having experienced incivility ([Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/from-functioning-to-flourishing/201707/why-are-our-workplaces-getting-ruder)). Groups whose conversation was dominated by one person were less collectively intelligent, and equal turn-taking predicted the group's collective intelligence ([Woolley et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20929725/)). Interrupters gain perceived status but lose likability, with a larger penalty for women ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/40470974/this-is-why-you-always-get-interrupted-at-work)). What counts as an interruption also varies by listener ([Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/?p=20602)). Across 5,660 employees in 19 countries, workers were less satisfied with supervisors who swore often ([Loughborough 2026](https://www.lboro.ac.uk/media-centre/press-releases/2026/july/swearing-boss-might-make-you-work-or-quit/)), yet workplace swearing is not always judged negatively ([Communication Studies](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/10510974.2011.638411)).

What gets rewarded is narrower and more concrete than "emotional intelligence" or "presence". EI correlates ρ ≈ .24–.30 with job performance ([O'Boyle et al. 2011](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1363388844153142912?lang=en)), but **self-reported EI's correlation of ρ = .29 falls to β = −.02 once personality, ability EI and self-efficacy are controlled** ([Joseph et al. 2015](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/why-does-self-reported-emotional-intelligence-predict-job-perform/)). "Executive presence" is a practitioner construct. In one survey, 67% of 268 senior executives named gravitas as its core, and women and people of color described the feedback they received about it as contradictory ([Coqual](https://coqual.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/26_executivepresence_keyfindings-1.pdf)). The ideal is also culturally specific. American leaders show more *excited* smiles than Chinese and Taiwanese leaders, and a nation's ideal affect predicts its leaders' smiles ([Tsai et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4760873)). On repair, Gottman's work holds that stable couples make frequent repair attempts such as owning part of the problem ([Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/research/)). Apologizing repairs trust better than denial after competence lapses ([Kim et al. 2004](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/context/lkcsb_research/article/3367/viewcontent/RemovingShadowSuspicion_afv.pdf)), and staying silent is a poor response ([Ferrin et al. 2007](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/context/lkcsb_research/article/3525/viewcontent/SilenceSpeaksVolumes_2007.pdf)). Whether early or late apologies work better is unresolved: later ones, given after the other party has had a say, raised satisfaction in one study, while another favored early apology ([J. Business & Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-015-9431-8)). No retrievable research measures how one outburst changes a reputation or how fast repair must follow.

Hiding feelings has its own price. Across 95 studies, "surface acting" (faking or suppressing a display) correlated **ρ ≈ .39–.48 with impaired well-being**. "Deep acting" (changing the feeling itself) was only weakly linked to strain and positively linked to emotional performance ([Hülsheger & Schewe 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21728441/)). Surface acting reaches exhaustion through effort and reduced felt authenticity ([Huppertz et al. 2020](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/why-do-emotional-labor-strategies-differentially-predict-exhausti/)). In a UK sample of 472 adults, workplace masking led to exhaustion in autistic, other neurodivergent and neurotypical groups alike, with suicidal ideation appearing specific to autistic participants ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10482295/)). On video, keeping cameras on raised daily fatigue, which in turn reduced employees' voice and engagement in meetings, and the effect was stronger for women and newer employees ([Shockley et al. 2021](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-fatiguing-effects-of-camera-use-in-virtual-meetings-a-within-/)).

| "I" language plus the other's view | Least hostile | — | Vignette study |
| Naming an external cause for frustration | Removes the gender penalty | — | One 2008 study |
| Apologizing for a competence lapse | Repairs trust better than denial or silence | — | Older lab studies |

## Lower arousal first, rehearse reappraisal later, and never coach a mask

Gross's process model sorts regulation strategies by where they act. A meta-analysis of 306 experimental comparisons found **perspective-taking the most effective tactic (d+ = 0.45)**, followed by reappraising the situation (0.36) and distraction (0.27). Concentrating on the emotion backfired (−0.26). Suppressing *expression* changed the outward display (0.32), but suppressing the *experience* did not work ([Webb, Miles & Sheeran 2012](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Dealing_with_feeling_a_meta-analysis_of_the_effectiveness_of_strategies_derived_from_the_process_model_of_emotion_regulation/23396432)). Suppression lowers what shows without lowering what is felt, and it impairs memory ([Gross 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12212647/)). It distracts suppressors during conversation, and secondary summaries report that it raises blood pressure in their conversation partners too ([Butler et al. 2003](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-social-consequences-of-expressive-suppression/)). It also predicts more negative first impressions and less social support ([Chervonsky & Hunt 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28080085)). A coach that rewards a calm-looking face is therefore training the costly strategy. That is the central design risk for Steady.

How intense the emotion is decides what is feasible. People prefer reappraisal for low-intensity situations and distraction or disengagement for high-intensity ones ([Sheppes et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3647109)). The strongest evidence for in-the-moment control favors lowering arousal. Across 154 studies (N = 10,189), **arousal-decreasing activities reduced anger and aggression (g = −0.63), while arousal-increasing ones did nothing (g = −0.02)**, and the effect held across settings and delivery formats ([Kjærvik & Bushman 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38518585/)). Effective techniques included deep breathing, relaxation and time-outs, and venting did not help ([Ohio State](https://news.osu.edu/breathe-dont-vent-turning-down-the-heat-is-key-to-managing-anger/)). Breathwork reduced stress (g = −0.35) across 12 RCTs ([Fincham et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9828383/)). Exhale-focused "cyclic sighing", practiced five minutes a day, beat mindfulness on mood, and the benefits grew with days of practice ([Balban et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/)). DBT's STOP skill (stop, take a step back, observe, proceed mindfully) is a ready-made micro-pause ([Linehan manual](https://www.guilford.com/add/linehan7_old/lin-p-4teaching.pdf?t=1)). The realistic in-meeting moves are a long exhale, a holding phrase, and unclenching the jaw and hands. Reappraisal and perspective-taking are better rehearsed before and after meetings, with only a light cue when the reaction is moderate.

Naming a feeling helps, provided the person does the naming. Labeling reduced distress about as much as reappraisal or distraction, even though participants did not believe it would work ([Lieberman et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21534661/)). In spider-phobic participants, labeling during exposure produced lower skin-conductance responses a week later ([Kircanski et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22902568/)). The evidence covers self-generated labels; no study has tested labels supplied by an app. Silent self-distancing, such as referring to oneself by name, improved performance under social stress ([Kross et al. 2014](https://rascl.berkeley.edu/assets/files/self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_how_you_do_it_matters.pdf)).

Habit reversal training is the closest template for what Steady is trying to do. Its core ingredients are awareness training, meaning learning to detect the behavior and its early precursors, and a competing response ([PubMed review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7593685/)). A secondary summary of older work reports that only *contingent* use helps, meaning the response is performed every time the urge appears, not on a schedule ([Wikipedia](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Habit_reversal_training)). The evidence comes from tics and body-focused habits, so applying it to emotional reactions is an analogy. Monitoring itself changes behavior: across 138 experiments, progress monitoring improved goal attainment (**d = 0.40**), more so when progress was physically recorded ([Harkin et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/)). The just-in-time adaptive intervention (JITAI) framework gives a vocabulary of decision points, tailoring variables and decision rules ([Nahum-Shani et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5364076)). Its best-known trial capped prompts at two a day to limit burden and found modest effects ([HeartSteps](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192907/)).

Feedback can also backfire. In a meta-analysis of 607 effects, feedback improved performance on average (d = .41) but **reduced performance in more than a third of cases, especially when it drew attention to the self rather than the task** ([Kluger & DeNisi 1996](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/)). Attention to repeated warnings drops sharply within days, while warnings that vary their appearance resist habituation ([MIS Quarterly 2018](https://aisel.aisnet.org/misq/vol42/iss2/3)). Self-compassion after a failure increased motivation to improve more than a self-esteem boost did ([Breines & Chen 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22645164/)). Between meetings, structured debriefs improve performance (d = .67) ([Tannenbaum & Cerasoli](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23516804/)). After-action reviews reach d = 0.79 and work best with **objective review media** ([Keiser & Arthur summary](https://qic-wd.org/node/596)). If-then implementation intentions raise goal attainment (d = 0.65) ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)) and have large effects on emotional outcomes ([Webb, Schweiger Gallo et al.](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/webb_schweiger_gallo_ea_2012.pdf)). Reappraisal training transfers poorly to daily life, except for distancing-style reappraisal ([Denny & Ochsner](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4096123)).

The case for gradually withdrawing prompts is real but unsettled. Prompt-fading procedures generally work in behavior analysis ([J. Developmental & Physical Disabilities](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10882-017-9575-8)). In motor learning, the "guidance hypothesis" predicts that feedback on every trial helps during practice but hurts retention, and an older meta-analysis supported it ([Marschall et al. 2007](https://orbilu.uni.lu/bitstream/10993/4589/1/Publikation_Marschall_Bund_Wiemeyer_2007_EJournal%20Bewegung%20und%20Training.PDF)). A preregistered meta-analysis found no support for timing-dependent feedback-frequency effects ([JEMS](https://www.scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/2529)), and one 2024 experiment found that feedback on 67% of trials beat lower rates ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933749)). Fading has to be built in and tested, not assumed.

The intended users raise the stakes. In one study, **55% of adults with ADHD reported emotional self-regulation problems more severe than 95% of controls** ([Surman et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4282137)). Mental-health apps produce small effects on average (g ≈ 0.26–0.28), and only g = 0.12 for PTSD ([Linardon et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10785982)). About 6.7% of app users deteriorate, a rate similar to controls ([npj Digital Medicine](https://doaj.org/article/56646abb0df94bc2b0fd9c7cc6828cc5)), and in a 2023 safety review, 35% of the 23 studies collected no safety data at all ([JMIR Mental Health](https://mental.jmir.org/2023/1/e47433)).

## Coaching tools that lasted stayed private, sparse and behavioral

Research prototypes point to the same design. The Rhema system on Google Glass flashed a single word such as "louder" for about 3 seconds, then stayed blank for about 20 seconds, and showed nothing when the speaker was doing well. Speakers preferred this **sparse scheme over continuous feedback** ([Rochester](https://www.rochester.edu/newscenter/wearable-technology-can-help-with-public-speaking-95552)). Meeting Mediator showed feedback privately on each participant's phone, which reduced overlapping speech "without distracting the subjects" ([MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/meeting-mediator-enhancing-group-collaboration-with-sociometric-feedback-2)). Among 5,179 support agents given real-time AI suggestions they could ignore, productivity rose 14% on average and **34% for novices**, and the authors note the tool may have produced worker learning ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161)).

The market history reads as a warning about emotion labels and surveillance. Microsoft retired Azure's emotion inference in 2022, citing the lack of scientific consensus on what "emotions" are ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/microsoft-removing-emotion-recognition-features-facial-recognition-tec-rcna35087)). Zoom's emotion-AI research drew an open letter from nearly 30 advocacy groups ([Fight for the Future](https://www.fightforthefuture.org/news/2022-05-10-letter-to-zoom/)). Amazon Halo's "Tone" feature analyzed the user's own voice on the phone to show "how you sound to others", which makes it Steady's closest precedent. A reviewer found it "seemed to misinterpret situations regularly" ([SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/lifestyle/gadgets/article/3099589/can-amazon-halo-can-tell-your-emotions-your-tone-voice-experts)), and Amazon discontinued Halo in 2023 ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2023/04/26/amazon-discontinuing-halo-wristband/)). Halo failed on usefulness and accuracy, not on privacy. Microsoft retired Teams Speaker Coach in August 2025 "after evaluating feedback and usage" ([MC1101903](https://mc.merill.net/message/MC1101903)), and Poised shut down in October 2026 ([Poised](https://poised.com/)). Yoodli survives by giving "private coaching nudges that only you can see" on pacing, filler words and tone ([Yoodli](https://yoodli.ai/use-cases/online-meetings)). Cogito's call-center nudges show how a coach turns into surveillance. Its alerts fed a supervisor dashboard, minimizing the window alerted the supervisor, the "empathy" cue pinged too often, and a shared laugh was flagged as talking over each other ([Santa Clara University case](https://scu.edu/ethics/focus-areas/internet-ethics/resources/sentio-ergo-sum)). Arousal is not stress, either: in about 800 students, a smartwatch stress score correlated "basically zero" with self-reported stress ([Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/new-study-finds-smartwatches-arent-that-good-at-measuring-stress-2000640868)).

The legal picture favors Steady's architecture and puts pressure on its wording. Article 5(1)(f) of the EU AI Act has prohibited AI that infers emotions from biometric data in the workplace since February 2025, with narrow medical and safety exceptions ([AI Act Service Desk](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-5)). The 2026 Digital Omnibus left that ban unchanged ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)). A suggested carve-out for personal training whose results are never shared with HR appears only in the Commission's non-binding guidelines, not in the Act's text ([Technology's Legal Edge](https://www.technologyslegaledge.com/2025/04/eu-ai-act-spotlight-on-emotional-recognition-systems-in-the-workplace/)). Recital 18 excludes the "mere detection of readily apparent expressions" such as a frown, but only provided the system does not infer emotional states ([Recital 18](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/recital-18)). Labels like "May read as frustrated" or "May sound angry" sit closer to emotion inference than "Brow lowered" does. In the US, the Seventh Circuit held in August 2026 that BIPA does not reach biometric data that stays on the user's device ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/seventh-circuit-holds-that-bipa-does-not-reach-biometric-data-that-remains-on-a-users-device)). In the Otter.ai litigation, wiretap claims survived dismissal because the vendor used recordings for its own commercial purposes ([Sheppard Mullin](https://www.sheppard.com/insights/blogs/when-ai-takes-notes-court-allows-privacy-claims-against-otterai-to-proceed)). Washington's My Health My Data Act defines health data broadly, including inferences, and gives consumers a private right to sue ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/resources/article/washington-my-health-my-data-act-overview)). The FTC continues to pursue unsubstantiated AI claims ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/operation-ai-comply-2-years-later-continued-enforcement)), and the FDA's January 2026 wellness guidance covers stress-management claims but not claims about disease ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/media/90652/download)).

## What Steady should change, in priority order

Steady already gets several hard things right, and these should be protected. It compares every cue with the user's own resting face, including at several head angles. That matches both the large differences between individuals and the way face tracking degrades as the head turns. It describes visible behavior rather than claiming to read feelings. It processes everything on the device and has no employer channel, which is the main lesson from Cogito and the strongest US legal position. It transcribes speech only while the user's own mouth is moving. It judges pitch over 5-second windows with a 2-second hold, so normal emphasis is ignored, and it requires loudness *and* sharpness before calling a voice raised, so leaning toward the microphone is not misread. It never asks users to smile more or flags low expressivity. Its long-turn nudge, "Ask a question", fits the turn-taking evidence.

The gaps sit in three places: the response Steady coaches, the outcome it measures, and the missing bridge from the app noticing to the user noticing. Today's nudges mostly ask the user to correct the display ("Soften your face", "Reset to neutral", "Bring them back level"). The session summary reports an average score computed as 100 × (1 − heat), which is highest when nothing shows. Nothing in the app helps users learn their own early warning signs, plan a different response, or need Steady less over time. One adaptive feature deserves scrutiny as well. Thresholds rise when a cue "keeps running high for you in meetings". That suppresses false alarms from an idiosyncratic face, but it also risks teaching the coach to tolerate the very habit the user is trying to break.

| Priority | Change | Why this order | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rewrite live nudges to cue arousal reduction and the user's own competing response; replace emotion words in labels with descriptions of behavior and intensity | Current copy coaches suppression, which is linked to memory loss, partner stress and burnout; labels claim emotions the signals cannot support, and that is where EU legal exposure sits | Well supported (suppression and arousal meta-analyses, habit reversal contingency, Recital 18); exact wording needs user testing |
| 2 | Replace the 0–100 calm score with recovery time, self-catches and plans used, in self-compassionate language | The score rewards masking and is self-focused evaluative feedback | Principle well supported (Kluger & DeNisi, Harkin, Breines & Chen); the specific metrics need validation |
| 3 | Add a self-catch tap and a two-minute post-meeting review that ends in an if-then plan; show the plan before the next meeting and cue its "then" live | This is the step that moves detection from the app to the person, which is the product's stated goal | Well supported (debriefs d = .67–.79, implementation intentions d = .65, habit reversal awareness training); untested for workplace emotion |
| 4 | Make live feedback private, sparse and silent when things are fine: discreet screen plus haptics by default, no full-screen red or mirroring face during meetings, a global cooldown and a per-meeting cap | A visible red screen and a mirroring face add self-focus and risk embarrassment; repeated alerts habituate | Mechanisms well supported (Rhema, Meeting Mediator, habituation research); exact caps need testing |
| 5 | Rebalance detectors: words as the main stance signal with a tiered, context-aware phrase list; voice treated as intensity with noise compensation; stricter eye-roll checks; adapt thresholds from user-marked errors rather than frequency | False alarms drove abandonment of Halo Tone and frustration with Cogito | Channel weighting well supported; implementation details need testing |
| 6 | Fade support on performance criteria, and verify skill in periodic no-nudge meetings | Independence is the stated goal, but the right schedule is unknown | Needs testing; fading evidence is contested |
| 7 | Guardrails: separate off switches for face, voice and words; a gentle mode; signposting that this is not therapy; a distress check; EU legal review; claims discipline; no employer features | Protects the users most likely to be misread and limits legal exposure | Well supported as risk control; the legal points are unsettled |

### Cue a practiced response, not a face fix

Every nudge should combine a neutral observation with an action that lowers arousal or carries out the user's plan, matched to intensity. A large, fast drift calls for an exhale or a pause. A moderate drift can carry a perspective-taking prompt, the strongest reappraisal tactic in the meta-analysis. Body-relaxation moves such as unclenching the jaw belong in the new copy, because releasing muscles is an arousal-lowering competing response rather than a mask. Instructions to rearrange the face do not. Voice labels should describe intensity relative to the user's own normal, since the acoustics cannot identify the emotion. Face labels should name the movement and, at most, its social reading in stance terms such as "can read as disagreement" or "can read as dismissive", not emotion terms such as "frustrated" or "angry". That change follows the science and also narrows exposure under Recital 18. Counsel should still review it, because a regulator could treat any product whose purpose is emotion regulation as inferring emotion.

| Cue | Today's nudge and label | Problem | Proposed direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scowl | "Brow is tightening. Soften your face." / "May read as frustrated" | Asks for a mask; names an emotion | "Brow tight. Long exhale." Then the user's plan, e.g. "Ask what they need." Label: "Brow lowered" |
| Tight lips | "Lips pressed tight. Relax your jaw." / "May read as tense or annoyed" | Jaw release is good; the label names emotions | "Jaw tight. Exhale and unclench." Label: "Lips pressed" |
| Smirk | "That may read as a smirk. Reset to neutral." | Asks for a mask | "One-sided smile. Pause. What's their side?" Label: "Can read as dismissive" |
| Eye roll | "Eyes rolling up. Bring them back level." | Asks for a mask | "Pause before replying. Try 'Help me understand.'" |
| Raised voice | "Voice is rising. Bring it down a notch." / "May sound angry" | Arousal is not anger; noise produces the same pattern | "Louder than your usual. Exhale before the next sentence." |
| Pitch | "Breathe out and lower your voice." / "May sound stressed" | Good action; the label is unsupported | Keep the action. Label: "Pitch above your usual" |
| Pace | "Slow down and pause." / "May sound anxious" | Good action; the evidence on the anxious voice is contradictory | Keep the action. Label: "Faster than your usual" |
| Sharp words | "May sound confrontational" plus a rewrite tip | Mostly sound | Favor "I" language plus the other's view, and naming the cause ("I'm frustrated because the deadline moved") |

### Measure recovery and self-catches, not calm

Replace the average score and "None. Nicely done." with what the goal actually requires: seconds from a nudge back to the user's normal range, self-catches logged *before* the app nudged, the ratio of self-catches to app nudges (the clearest sign that the skill is transferring), plans used, and repair moves. Write summaries the way the self-compassion and feedback evidence recommends, for example "Tough meeting. You came back in 40 seconds at minute 12, and caught yourself once before Steady did". Avoid streaks that punish a lapse. A still face should earn nothing on its own.

### Build the bridge from app noticing to self-noticing

Add a one-tap or keyboard "I caught it" action that works in discreet mode. Physically recording progress strengthens monitoring, and habit reversal depends on the person detecting precursors. After each meeting, run a two-minute review. Show one to three flagged moments on a timeline, since objective review media are what make after-action reviews work. Ask what set the moment off, what the user noticed first in their body, what they did, and what they will try next time. Let the user pick a word for what they felt, which puts the labeling benefit in their hands rather than the model's. End the review with an if-then plan stored on the device, such as "If Sam interrupts me while I'm presenting, I'll exhale and say 'Let me finish this point.'" Show that plan before the next meeting and cue its "then" step live, so the nudge becomes the contingent trigger habit reversal requires. A short daily practice of exhale-focused breathing between meetings would make the live cue trigger a rehearsed response rather than a new instruction.

### Make live feedback private, sparse and quiet when things are fine

A full-screen shift from navy to red is an alarm signal, and anyone who can see the device sees it, whether an in-person colleague or viewers of a shared screen. The mirroring cartoon face adds a second self-view in a setting where self-view has been tied to negative affect. Make the existing discreet mode plus vibration the meeting default, and move the color history and face illustrations to the post-meeting review. Today each cue has its own cooldown (9 seconds by default), so several different cues can fire back to back. Add a global gap between nudges, using Rhema's roughly 20 seconds of silence as a starting point to test. Add a per-meeting cap, reserve the strongest signal for the largest deviations, and use a small set of nudge variations to slow habituation.

### Rebalance the detectors toward what each channel can tell

Make words the main signal of stance and tier the phrase list by evidence. At the top go blaming "you" statements with absolutes ("you always", "you never", "your fault"), then contempt markers ("obviously", "who cares", "if you say so"), then defensive deflection ("not my fault", "don't blame me"), then dismissal ("whatever"). Demote or require context for phrases that are ordinary or appropriately assertive at work, such as "that's not true", "you need to", "we tried that" and "I was told to". Remove "let me finish" from the blaming list entirely, because it is exactly the kind of assertive holding phrase a good if-then plan uses. Treat profanity aimed at a person differently from frustrated swearing, since workplace reactions to swearing vary. Route first-person absolutes ("I always ruin this") to a self-compassion nudge, because they signal distress rather than hostility. Credit repair phrases ("help me understand", "that's on me", "I see it differently") in the summary.

For voice, estimate background noise from the non-speech frames the voice detector already identifies, and discount loudness and sharpness when the room is loud. Do not add an "anxious voice" flag. For the eye-roll cue, require a sweep up and a return, not just a raised gaze, because looking up on its own is ambiguous. Confirm that no cue depends on which side of the face moves; MediaPipe's left and right refer to the subject's own face, and a mirrored input swaps them ([MediaPipe docs](https://developers.google.com/edge/mediapipe/solutions/vision/face_landmarker)). Add a "that was wrong" button and let those marks, rather than how often a cue runs high, drive threshold changes. Do not add interruption or slow-response cues unless they account for network latency.

### Fade toward independence, and prove it in no-nudge meetings

A five-stage path is a reasonable hypothesis to test. Start with frequent nudges paired with reviews. Move to "bandwidth" nudges that fire only on large drifts, leaving smaller ones for the user to catch. Next, ask users to rate a meeting before they see Steady's data, which builds self-calibration. Then switch to summary-only with live cues reserved for big deviations. Finally, run periodic full-feedback check-ups. Stages should advance on performance, such as the self-catch ratio and recovery time, not on the calendar, and step back after a relapse or before a meeting the user marks as high stakes. Because the motor-learning evidence conflicts, the test that matters is behavior in *no-nudge* meetings, which is the equivalent of a retention test, not behavior while nudges are firing. Several further ideas deserve controlled comparison rather than immediate rollout. They include extra sensitivity in a meeting's opening minutes, a silent "name it" prompt during the meeting versus labeling only afterward, a combined "slow, flat voice plus sharp words" signature for sarcasm and cold anger, repair prompts after a flagged moment ("own your part", "name the cause", "ask what they need"), and a reassurance that a reaction is probably less visible than it feels. Steady makes many decisions inside each meeting, which suits micro-randomized trials. Because no data leaves the device, those trials will need on-device logging and an opt-in export that the user controls.

### Guardrails that protect the users most likely to be misread

Users with regulation difficulties overlap heavily with ADHD, autism and anxiety. They need separate off switches for face, voice and words, including words-only and audio-only modes. People who startle easily need a gentle mode without chimes or vibration bursts. Thresholds should never be stricter for any demographic group; users can raise sensitivity themselves if they choose. Steady should state plainly that it is a skills coach, not therapy. It should point people with persistent difficulties to DBT skills groups, CBT for anger, or ADHD care, and ask a one-question distress or shame check after sessions as a deterioration signal. Keep the architecture free of dashboards and team plans, and write terms that forbid employer-mandated use. Get EU legal advice on whether to switch to behavior-only labels, offer a words-only mode in the EU, or geofence face and voice cues there. Make no accuracy percentages and no "reduces outbursts" claims until Steady's own trials support them.

## Conclusion

The case against reading emotions from faces turns out to support Steady's core idea, as long as it is narrowed. Expression is an unreliable window into feeling, but perception is fast, consequential and skewed toward the negative, so a private mirror of *how a moment lands* stands on firm ground. The same science says the user, not the model, is the authority on what they feel. That is why naming the feeling, catching it oneself, and planning a different response belong at the center of the product, with detection as scaffolding. The single most important reframe is that Steady succeeds when it becomes unnecessary. The numbers to watch are self-catches that come before the app nudges and conduct in meetings without nudges, not calm faces during meetings with them. That inverts the usual engagement metrics and argues for designing fading in from the start.

Two open questions should shape the roadmap. No study has tested live emotion-regulation cues for adults in real workplace meetings, so Steady's central mechanism is unproven. Its privacy-first design makes it well placed to produce that evidence, provided evaluation is built on the device and shared only by the user's choice. The fairness evidence also points to a constructive option that avoids asking already-penalized users to suppress more. Penalties for women's and Black women's anger run through trait attributions, and an external explanation removed one such penalty. So "name the cause" scripts and better-structured content can narrow perception gaps without making anyone hide more of themselves, which is a more equitable lever than any threshold setting.
